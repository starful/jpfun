## Why Divers Keep Coming Back to the Twin Boulders

Matsuzaki sits on the western coast of the Izu Peninsula, a stretch of Shizuoka Prefecture that gets far less dive traffic than the eastern side around Ito or the southern tip near Mikomoto. That relative quiet is exactly what makes the Twin Boulders special. The site is named for two large rock formations — weathered granite masses that rise from a sandy-rubble bottom and sit close enough together that a single dive can weave between both. Unlike a wall dive or a drift site, the Twin Boulders reward divers who like to explore terrain: swim-throughs, overhangs, crevices packed with nudibranchs, and boulder faces thick with soft coral and encrusting sponge.

This is a site for divers who want variety in a single dive rather than one long feature. You get topography (the boulders themselves), macro life in the cracks and ledges, and mid-water interest from schooling fish that use the rocks as a current break. Because the formations break up the flow of water moving along the coast, they create sheltered pockets on their lee side that are noticeably calmer than the open exposure just a few meters away — useful for newer divers who want a taste of temperate-water diving without full exposure to swell.

It's also a good match for underwater photographers. The boulders' vertical relief, combined with decent visibility on calm days, gives wide-angle shooters a foreground subject with depth, while the nooks and crevices are a magnet for macro subjects — nudibranchs, gobies, and the occasional octopus tucked into a hole. Divers who've done the more famous Izu sites (Kawana, Futo, or Osezaki) often describe Matsuzaki as a chance to dive without a crowd of buddy teams stacked on the same swim-through.

## Site Conditions: Layout, Depth, and What You'll See

The Twin Boulders sit in a temperate coastal zone typical of western Izu — expect a mixed bottom of sand, gravel, and broken rock leading up to the two main pinnacles. Depths at the site generally range from around 5 meters near the shallow tops of the boulders down to 18–20 meters at the surrounding seabed, which makes it workable for both open water divers sticking to the shallower profile and more experienced divers who want to circle the base of the formations.

Visibility is genuinely seasonal here. In late spring through early summer you can expect 8–15 meters on a good day, while typhoon season (roughly August through September) can stir up the water and cut that in half after a swell. Winter months, once the water cools, often bring the clearest conditions of the year — sometimes exceeding 15 meters — because plankton density drops and the coast sees fewer storm systems. This is a common pattern along temperate Japanese coastlines: cold, clear winter water traded for warmer, murkier summer water.

Marine life reflects that temperate character rather than a tropical reef. Look for schools of striped jack and yellowtail moving through the gap between the two boulders, rockfish and scorpionfish tucked against the granite, and a healthy population of nudibranchs and other macro life in the shaded crevices, especially from late winter into spring when many species are most active. Soft corals and hydroids coat the boulder faces in patches, and it's common to find moray eels denned in the deeper cracks near the base. Because the site isn't heavily dived, marine life tends to be less skittish than at more trafficked spots.

Currents can run along the boulder faces depending on tide, so this isn't a completely lazy dive — local dive operators typically brief a route that uses the boulders themselves as current shelter, working from the lee side outward. It's a good idea to dive this site with a shop that knows the local tide tables rather than trying to plan the timing yourself.

## Best Season and How to Reach Matsuzaki

The diving season at the Twin Boulders effectively runs year-round, since Izu's water temperature rarely drops low enough to force a full shutdown, but conditions vary a lot by month. Water temperature typically peaks in the 24–27°C range from August into early October, making a 5mm wetsuit comfortable, while winter (January–March) can drop to around 15–17°C, calling for a drysuit or thick semi-dry setup if you want to stay in the water for a full two-tank day. Many repeat divers actually favor the shoulder seasons — April to June and October to November — for the balance of manageable water temperature and consistently better visibility than peak summer.

Typhoon season (August–September) is the main wildcard. Swell generated by storms passing well offshore can still churn up the coast around Matsuzaki even when the weather locally looks fine, so it's worth checking a marine forecast (not just a land weather app) before booking, and building a flexible day or two into your trip if you're traveling from far away specifically to dive this site.

Getting to Matsuzaki takes a bit more effort than the more built-up dive towns on Izu's east coast, which is part of why it stays quiet. From Tokyo, the most practical route is by car: take the Tomei Expressway toward Numazu, then follow Route 136 down the western side of the peninsula through Dogashima to Matsuzaki, a drive of roughly 3 to 3.5 hours depending on traffic. Without a car, the train-and-bus route runs via the JR Tokaido Shinkansen or limited express to Mishima or Shuzenji, then a connecting bus down the west coast to Matsuzaki — plan for around 4 hours door to door, and check bus schedules in advance since they're infrequent outside peak tourist periods. If you're flying in, Shizuoka Airport is the nearest option, followed by a roughly 2.5-hour drive or bus transfer to Matsuzaki; Haneda and Narita are viable but add significant transfer time compared to a direct Tokyo departure.

Most visiting divers base themselves in Matsuzaki town itself or nearby Dogashima, both of which have small dive shops that run boat trips out to the Twin Boulders and other local sites.

## Booking, Gear, and Practical Etiquette for the Trip

Because Matsuzaki isn't a mass-market dive hub, it's worth booking through a local shop in advance rather than showing up and hoping for a same-day slot — most operations run small boats and cap group sizes, and popular weekend dates in the shoulder seasons can fill up. A local guide is also genuinely useful here: the Twin Boulders' current patterns and best swim-through routes aren't obvious on a first visit, and shops that dive the site regularly will time the trip around slack tide for the calmest conditions.

On gear, plan around the season rather than assuming a single wetsuit will cover your whole trip. A 5mm full wetsuit is generally fine for summer and early autumn diving, but if you're visiting in winter or early spring, a drysuit or heavy semi-dry with a hood and gloves will make a real difference in comfort, especially on a two-tank day. Most local shops rent both wetsuits and drysuits, but availability in the right size is more limited than at major dive resorts, so reserve gear rental when you book the dive rather than assuming it on arrival.

A dive computer and a delayed surface marker buoy (DSMB) are worth having even if the shop supplies rental gear, since boulder-and-current sites like this reward being able to track your own depth and signal your position clearly if you surface away from the boat. A dive light is genuinely useful too — the crevices and undercuts around the boulders are where most of the interesting macro life hides, and a light brings out color that ambient light washes out at depth.

Etiquette-wise, this is a site where good buoyancy control matters more than at an open sand-bottom dive, since the boulder faces are covered in soft coral, sponge, and hydroids that are easily damaged by fin contact or a stray tank. Keep your kit streamlined and be deliberate moving through the swim-throughs rather than finning through blind. Because the site sees relatively light dive traffic, it's also an easy place to leave a good impression — no crowding other buddy teams in the same crevice, and packing out anything you bring along, including biodegradable sunscreen containers.

For food and stay, Matsuzaki town has a handful of small ryokan and guesthouses along the coast, many of which cater specifically to divers and surfers and can store gear or rinse tanks overnight. The town is also known regionally for fresh seafood — look for set meals featuring local sazae (turban shell) and hamachi, a nice way to close out a dive day before the drive or bus back toward Tokyo.
