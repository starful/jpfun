## Why Tokyoites Quietly Claim Nasu as Their Summer Refuge

Nasu Highlands sits on the skirts of Mount Chausu, an active stratovolcano still venting sulfurous steam from its Musen-kyo fumarole field, and that geology is exactly why campers come. The plateau sits roughly 700 to 1,000 meters above sea level, which knocks 6 to 8 degrees Celsius off the stifling humidity of the Kanto plain in July and August. Families from Saitama and Tokyo treat it as a heat-refuge weekend rather than a wilderness expedition — the drive from central Tokyo is short enough to leave after breakfast and have a tent pitched before lunch. What makes Nasu different from other Kanto highland campgrounds is the layering of activities within a five-kilometer radius: dairy farms with hand-churned ice cream, a ropeway that climbs to the crater rim, half a dozen distinct onsen springs each with different mineral profiles, and a former Imperial villa turned public park. Campers here are less interested in deep backcountry solitude and more in a basecamp that lets them bounce between forest trail, farm stand, and hot spring in the same afternoon. It's also historically significant as one of the birthplaces of Japan's postwar camping culture — the area's campgrounds date back to the 1960s tourism boom that followed the completion of the Nasu Highland Line toll road.

## Reading the Plateau: Campsite Layout and Ground Conditions

The camping zone spreads across a series of terraced clearings cut into mixed larch and Japanese oak forest, generally split into three tiers by elevation. The lower tier, closest to the access road, has flatter, more compacted volcanic loam that drains fast after rain — this is where car campers and first-timers with rental tents should aim. The middle tier is shadier, with more exposed tree roots and a slight grade, favored by hammock campers and those bringing their own multi-day tents who want privacy screening from the forest canopy. The upper tier, closer to the tree line, is windier and cooler at night (expect single digits Celsius even in August), and it's where the view opens up toward the Nasu range's ridgeline — worth the extra rope-staking effort for sunrise light. Soil here is volcanic andosol, dark and soft, which means stakes go in easily but can pull loose in the frequent afternoon thunderstorms typical of a highland microclimate, so bring extra guy-lines and wide stake heads rather than thin nail stakes. Free-standing tents are genuinely advantageous at Nasu for this reason. Water sources on-site are treated tap water at communal blocks rather than natural streams, since the volcanic runoff further upslope carries elevated sulfur content and is not potable. Firewood is sold bundled at the site office; foraging deadfall is discouraged because much of the surrounding forest is designated erosion-control woodland tied to the volcano's sabo (sediment-control) infrastructure.

## Season Window, Getting In, and the Logistics That Matter

The comfortable camping season runs from late April, once overnight frost risk passes, through early November, when the larch needles turn gold and temperatures drop sharply after dark. Peak season is mid-July through August, when Kanto residents flee the lowland heat — book at least three weeks ahead for Obon week (mid-August) or weekends will be full. Winter camping is technically possible in shoulder pockets but requires cold-rated gear, since night lows can hit -5°C even before the snow season proper begins in December. By car, Nasu Highlands is about two and a half hours from central Tokyo via the Tohoku Expressway, exiting at Nasu Kogen IC, followed by a scenic 20-minute climb up Nasu Kogen Road. Without a car, take the Tohoku Shinkansen to Nasushiobara Station (roughly 70 minutes from Tokyo), then transfer to a local bus bound for Nasu Onsen or the ropeway — buses run on a reduced schedule outside peak season, so check timetables before relying on the last connection back down. A car is strongly recommended once you're up there, since the campground, onsen villages, and farm attractions are spread thinly across the plateau with infrequent local transit between them. Note that the last stretch of road toward the Chausu ropeway station can close temporarily in bad weather or early/late season snow, so check road status if you're camping into November or arriving in early spring.

## Where to Eat, Where to Soak, and Small Things Worth Knowing

For food, skip cooking every meal and walk or drive to one of the highland's dairy farms — several sell fresh milk, yogurt, and soft-serve made same-day from their own herds, and a couple run simple lunch counters with grilled Tochigi wagyu skewers that make a strong afternoon break from campsite cooking. In the Nasu Onsen town cluster below the campground, small izakaya serve yuba (tofu skin) dishes, a regional specialty tied to Tochigi's soy-farming tradition, alongside standard grilled fish sets. For soaking, Nasu's onsen are unusually varied for one area: Shika-no-yu is a rustic, centuries-old bathhouse with milky sulfur water believed to have been discovered by a wounded deer (hence the name), while other springs nearby run clear and iron-rich — worth visiting two different ones if you're staying multiple nights, since the mineral content genuinely differs spring to spring. If tent life isn't for the whole group, several highland lodges and pensions sit within a ten-minute drive of the campground and will hold a room for late arrivals if you call ahead. Practical tips: pack a proper rain layer regardless of forecast, since Chausu generates its own localized weather and afternoon showers arrive with little warning; bring bear bells or a small radio for early-morning or dusk walks, as Asiatic black bears are occasionally sighted on the upper forest trails; and keep food sealed in the provided lockers at night, since raccoon dogs (tanuki) and foxes are persistent and skilled at unzipping loose tent vestibules. Mobile signal is reliable at the lower and middle campsite tiers but patchy near the ropeway station, so download offline maps before you climb.
