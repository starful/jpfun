## Why Divers Nickname This Reef After a Royal Garden

Locals started calling this patch of reef off Zamami's western coast "Shikina-en" long before it appeared on any dive shop map, and the comparison to Naha's famous Ryukyu royal garden is not an exaggeration. Massive table corals stack in tiers like manicured terraces, sand channels wind between coral heads the way garden paths wind between ponds, and schools of tropical fish move through the structure with the same unhurried elegance as koi in a palace moat. It is one of the most photogenic reefscapes in the Kerama Islands, and it earns that reputation through structure rather than depth — this is a site built for wide-angle photography and slow, deliberate exploration rather than adrenaline.

This spot is for divers who want to actually look at coral, not just swim past it. Open Water divers on their first Kerama trip will find the topography forgiving: gentle slopes, sheltered sand patches for buoyancy checks, and enough visual interest in the shallows that there is little temptation to chase depth. Underwater photographers and macro hunters will find the coral gardens dense enough to spend an entire 60-minute dive within a 30-meter radius. It also works well as a second or third dive of the day, when nitrogen loading makes a shallow, relaxed profile more appealing than another deep wall dive elsewhere on Zamami. What makes it distinct from the more famous walls at Ama or the drift sites off Aka Island is the sheer density and variety of hard coral cover — Kerama's reefs took a hard hit from bleaching events in past decades, and this particular garden is one of the healthier recovery zones, which is exactly why the dive community has protected its exact GPS marks rather than publishing them widely.

## Reading the Reef: Coral Structure, Fish Life, and Typical Conditions

The site sits in a bay pocket that breaks much of the open-ocean swell, so conditions here are noticeably calmer than the exposed points around Zamami and neighboring Geruma. Typical depths run from 5 meters on the shallow coral plateau down to about 18 meters where the garden tapers into a sand-and-rubble slope, making it accessible across a wide range of certification levels when guided properly.

**Coral structure**: Expect extensive stands of table Acropora, branching staghorn thickets, and rounded Porites boulder colonies that create swim-throughs and small caves at the 8–12 meter band. The layout genuinely does resemble terraced garden beds, with sandy "paths" cutting between coral clusters — useful for divers still working on buoyancy, since you can navigate without hovering directly over fragile branches.

**Fish life**: The coral density supports thick clouds of damselfish and anthias that part and reform as you swim through, along with resident parrotfish grazing the reef flat, moray eels tucked into boulder crevices, and regular sightings of hawksbill turtles cruising the shallows to feed. Reef sharks are occasionally spotted at the deeper sand edge in early morning dives, and during the warmer months schools of yellowtail barracuda sometimes stage over the drop-off.

**Current and visibility**: Current is generally mild to negligible inside the bay, which is precisely why this site is favored for training dives, photography, and dives with less-experienced buddies. Visibility typically runs 15–25 meters in stable summer weather but can drop after heavy rain runoff from the island, so it's worth asking your shop about recent conditions before booking a slot specifically for photography. Water temperature ranges from around 22°C in winter to 29°C in peak summer, so a 3mm wetsuit is comfortable most of the year and a 5mm or hooded vest is worth considering for December–March trips.

## When to Dive It and How to Actually Get There

**Best season**: The Kerama Islands dive year runs essentially year-round, but the sweet spot for this particular site is April through November, when calm seas and warm water make for long, relaxed bottom times. July and August bring the best visibility and the busiest boats, so early morning charters are worth requesting. Late spring (April–May) and early autumn (October–November) offer a strong balance of good conditions and thinner crowds, plus a real chance of encountering migrating whale sharks or, in winter months just offshore, humpback whales on the surface crossing. Typhoon season overlaps with summer, so always build a one-day buffer into your itinerary between August and October in case a storm cancels a boat day.

**Getting to Zamami Island**: Nearly every diver's journey starts at Naha Airport (OKA) on Okinawa's main island, which has direct flights from Tokyo, Osaka, Fukuoka, and several international hubs including Taipei and Hong Kong. From the airport, take a taxi or the Yui Rail monorail to Naha Port / Tomari Port (泊港), roughly 20–30 minutes depending on traffic. From Tomari Port, high-speed ferries (Queen Zamami) reach Zamami Island in about 50 minutes, while the slower car ferry (Ferry Zamami) takes about 90 minutes and costs less. Booking ferry tickets a few days ahead is strongly recommended in peak summer, as sailings sell out.

**Once on Zamami**: Most dive shops are clustered around Zamami Port and Ama Village, within walking distance or a short shuttle ride of the ferry terminal. Shops running trips to the Shikina-en coral garden typically depart by boat directly from Zamami Port, with the site itself only a 10–15 minute ride from the harbor — making it one of the more time-efficient dive sites on the island's schedule, ideal if you're squeezing in a two-tank morning before an afternoon ferry back to Naha.

## Booking, Gear, and Reef Etiquette for a Better Dive Day

**Booking your dive**: Reserve through a Zamami-based shop rather than a Naha-based operator bundling day trips — local shops know this site's current window-of-opportunity conditions and can adjust the dive order around tides. Most operators run two-tank morning trips with a surface interval back at the shop, and many can arrange nitrox on request for longer bottom times in the shallow coral zone. If you're staying overnight on Zamami (highly recommended over a rushed day trip), book both your ferry and your guesthouse or minshuku well ahead during Golden Week, Obon (mid-August), and summer weekends, when the island's limited accommodation fills quickly.

**Gear checklist**: A 3mm full or shorty wetsuit covers most of the season; bring reef-safe, mineral-based sunscreen only, since this is a designated conservation-priority reef where chemical sunscreens are discouraged by local operators. Bring your own dive computer and a compact camera rig if you have one — the shallow, well-lit coral terraces here are some of the best natural-light photography conditions in the Kerama chain, so you won't need strobes for most shots above 10 meters. Booties and reef-friendly fins help avoid accidental kicks against the shallow coral plateau near the entry point.

**Etiquette and conservation**: This reef is actively recovering from past bleaching events, so buoyancy discipline matters more here than at hardier sites — keep fins and gauges tucked, avoid resting on coral even briefly, and follow your guide's line through the "garden paths" rather than cutting across coral heads. Many Zamami shops participate in coral monitoring and restoration programs and welcome divers who want to learn more or contribute a small donation toward reef recovery. Do not touch or chase turtles, and give resident moray eels their space in crevices rather than provoking a reaction for photos.

**Food and stay**: After diving, Zamami Village has a handful of small izakaya and cafés near the port serving fresh Okinawan fare — try the local Kerama beni-imo (purple sweet potato) desserts and fresh-caught fish teishoku sets. Guesthouses and minshuku on the island offer simple, comfortable rooms, often with breakfast included, and staying overnight lets you catch the very first boat out to the coral gardens before day-trip crowds arrive from Naha — the single best way to have this reef largely to yourself.
