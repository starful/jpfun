## Who Shikine Island Reef Dive Is For

Shikine Island sits in the Izu Islands chain, administratively part of Tokyo yet geologically and visually closer to Okinawa than to the capital's skyline. It is the kind of place that surprises first-time visitors: warm Kuroshio Current water pushes past the island for much of the year, feeding hard coral gardens, schools of tropical fish, and visibility that regularly exceeds 15 to 20 meters. That combination — Tokyo-adjacent logistics with subtropical reef conditions — is what sets Shikine apart from the more current-battered, temperate dive sites elsewhere in the Izu chain like Oshima or Kozushima.

This spot works for a wide range of divers. Open-water certified divers looking for a relaxed reef swim will find shallow, coral-dotted coves close to the harbor. Intermediate and advanced divers who want drift diving over current-scoured reef walls, encounters with larger pelagic visitors, and boat dives to offshore rock formations will find plenty to keep them engaged across a multi-day trip. Underwater photographers are especially well served: the clarity here makes wide-angle reefscapes and macro subjects on the coral heads both realistic goals in the same day.

It is not the right destination if you want a single quick dive squeezed into a day trip from central Tokyo — the ferry and lodging logistics make Shikine a proper overnight-plus trip. But for anyone building a Izu Islands dive itinerary, or looking for a reef-diving base that doesn't require a flight to Okinawa, Shikine is one of the most efficient options in the region.

## Dive Site Conditions and Layout

Shikine Island's dive sites cluster around a handful of coves and points accessible either by short boat rides from the harbor or, for a few sites, directly by shore entry. The island's small size means dive operators can pick whichever side is calmest on a given day, which matters because swell and wind direction here can change conditions quickly between the north and south coastlines.

- **Shore and cove dives**: The harbor-adjacent coves offer sandy-bottom entries leading into coral patches at 5–12 meters, ideal for check dives, refresher dives, and less experienced divers. Coral cover here includes branching and table corals along with anemone clusters that host resident clownfish and anemonefish species found further south in Japan.
- **Boat dives to reef points**: Slightly further out, rocky points drop from 10 meters down past 25–30 meters, with walls and boulder fields covered in soft coral and sponges. These sites see periodic current, which is exactly what draws in larger reef fish, schooling jacks, and occasional visits from rays.
- **Visibility and water character**: Because Shikine sits directly in the path of the Kuroshio Current, visibility is consistently among the best in the Izu Islands, often 15–25 meters in settled conditions. The trade-off is that current can pick up without much warning at exposed points, so guided boat dives are strongly preferred over independent shore diving at anything but the sheltered coves.
- **Marine life**: Expect hard and soft coral coverage, schools of damselfish and butterflyfish, moray eels tucked into rock crevices, and — particularly from late summer into autumn — visits from larger pelagic species riding the warm current. Divers doing multiple days often report different life on the same site depending on tide and current direction.

Dive operators on the island run small-group boat trips, typically two dives per morning session, and can tailor site selection to certification level and conditions on the day. Because Shikine is a small island with limited operator capacity, groups tend to be intimate rather than the large cattle-boat operations found at bigger dive destinations.

## Best Season and How to Get There

**Season**: Shikine Island's dive season runs roughly from late April through October, with the clearest water and warmest sea temperatures from July through September, when the Kuroshio Current pushes visibility and water temperature to their annual peak (often 24–28°C at the surface). Late spring (May–June) offers good visibility with fewer crowds but cooler water, better suited to a 5mm wetsuit or drysuit for longer dives. Typhoon season overlaps with the warmest months (August–September), so build a buffer day or two into any late-summer trip in case ferries are cancelled. Winter diving is possible for cold-water-experienced divers but water temperatures drop into the high teens and fewer operators run regular trips.

**Access**: Shikine Island has no airport, so the trip starts either from Tokyo or from Shimoda on the Izu Peninsula.

- **From Tokyo (Takeshiba Pier)**: High-speed jetfoils operated by Tokai Kisen run to Shikine Island in roughly 3 to 3.5 hours. Overnight large ferries are also available and take around 8 to 9 hours, arriving early morning — a popular option for divers who want to sleep through the crossing and start diving the same day.
- **From Shimoda**: Shimoda, at the southern tip of the Izu Peninsula, is a shorter jump-off point. Ferries and jetfoils from Shimoda to Shikine Island run in roughly 1.5 to 2 hours depending on the vessel and routing.
- **Getting to Shimoda**: From central Tokyo, take the JR/Izukyu limited express (around 2.5 to 3 hours direct from Tokyo Station) or drive down the Izu Peninsula via the Izu Skyline and coastal routes (roughly 3 hours from central Tokyo depending on traffic).
- **On the island**: Shikine Island is small enough to get around by scooter rental, bicycle, or on foot; most dive shops are within walking distance of the ferry port and offer pickup for gear-laden divers.

Because ferry schedules are limited and weather-dependent, always check sailing status a day or two ahead, especially in typhoon season, and build at least one buffer day into the itinerary if flight or work schedules are inflexible.

## Practical Tips for Booking, Gear, and Your Stay

**Booking dives**: Reserve with a Shikine Island dive shop at least a few weeks ahead during the July–September peak, since boat capacity is limited and popular weekends fill quickly. Most shops offer full rental gear packages (BCD, regulator, wetsuit, tank, weights) so it's entirely possible to fly — or ferry — in with just a mask and dive computer. If you're not yet certified, several shops run PADI Open Water and Discover Scuba Diving sessions using the same sheltered coves described above.

**Gear notes**: A 5mm wetsuit is comfortable for most of the April–October season; a hooded vest or thicker wetsuit is worth considering in May–June and October when water temperature dips. Bring your own mask and a reef-safe sunscreen — the coral cover here is a genuine ecological asset, and operators actively discourage sunscreens with oxybenzone and octinoxate. A dive light is useful even for daytime dives in the moray-filled crevices at the deeper boat sites.

**Reef etiquette**: Shikine's coral coverage is healthier than many mainland Japan dive sites precisely because of careful buoyancy control and no-touch norms enforced by local operators — keep fins and gauges tucked in on drift dives, and never stand on coral heads even briefly while adjusting gear.

**Stay and food**: Shikine Island has a small number of minshuku (family-run guesthouses) and a handful of pensions, many of which cater specifically to divers and can arrange gear storage and early breakfasts before boat departures. Book lodging alongside your dive package where possible, since availability is tight in peak season. For food, the island's small eateries lean heavily on the day's fish catch — grilled or as sashimi — alongside simple set meals; there are no convenience stores comparable to mainland Japan, so pack any specific snacks or medications you rely on.

**Onsen bonus**: Shikine Island is also known for its free open-air seaside hot spring baths, a genuinely rare pairing with world-class reef diving — plan a soak after your last dive of the day to round out the trip.
