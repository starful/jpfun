## A Muck-Diving Site for Patient Eyes, Not Wide-Angle Chasers

Iriomote Minato sits right at the harbor entrance on the southern side of Iriomote Island, in Japan's far-flung Yaeyama archipelago. It is not a site that will impress you with dramatic drop-offs or big schooling fish, and that is exactly the point. This is a critter site, built for divers who want to spend forty minutes staring at eight square meters of rubble and silt until something with eyes disguises itself as a rock.

If your idea of a great dive involves manta rays or wall-hugging currents, look elsewhere on Iriomote — the island has plenty of that too. But if you're the kind of diver who gets genuinely excited about finding a juvenile harlequin filefish tucked into a blade of seagrass, or a pair of ornate ghost pipefish drifting motionless above soft coral, Minato is the dive that will stay in your logbook long after the "big" dives fade.

What makes this site distinct is its location: a working harbor mouth with a mixed bottom of sand, silt, rubble, and scattered debris. That unglamorous substrate is a magnet for bottom-dwelling and camouflage species that avoid clean coral reef. Local Iriomote dive guides — mostly small, owner-operated shops based out of Ohara or Funaura port — treat Minato as their go-to muck site precisely because the mess is the habitat. Divers who've done muck diving in Lembeh or Anilao will recognize the vibe immediately; divers who haven't will get a crash course in why "boring-looking bottom" and "incredible biodiversity" are not contradictions.

## Site Layout and What's Actually Down There

Minato is a shallow, low-current site, generally sitting in the 5 to 15 meter range, which makes it forgiving on air consumption and ideal for long, slow-paced dives. There's no reef wall to follow here — instead, guides work a loose grid pattern across sand flats, patches of rubble, scattered rope and rope debris near the harbor pilings, and small isolated coral bommies that punctuate the otherwise flat bottom. Visibility tends to run murkier than Iriomote's outer reef sites, often in the 5 to 10 meter range, occasionally less after rain runoff from the island's rivers — but that reduced clarity is part of what keeps the muck ecosystem intact.

The critter list is the real draw. Regulars report:

- **Pipefish** — ornate ghost pipefish, robust ghost pipefish, and several *Halicampus* species tucked against algae and rubble
- **Frogfish** — painted frogfish and juvenile warty frogfish, often motionless for days in the same spot, which is why guides who dive here weekly can walk you straight to them
- **Nudibranchs** — a rotating cast depending on season, from tiny *Flabellina* species to larger *Chromodoris* and *Phyllidia*
- **Seahorses** — occasional pygmy and common seahorse sightings, usually spotted by guides who know exactly which gorgonian or seagrass patch to check
- **Juvenile fish** — this is a nursery zone, so look for juvenile batfish, filefish, and boxfish in their odd, still-forming color phases
- **Mimics and oddities** – flamboyant cuttlefish have turned up here, along with mantis shrimp burrows and the occasional blue-ringed octopus, which should be observed from a very respectful distance and never touched

Because the site rewards slow, methodical searching rather than fin-kicking around, guides typically brief divers to stay close, move in short controlled bursts, and let the guide do the spotting. This is very much a "follow closely and look where they point" dive rather than a free-swim exploration.

## When to Go and How to Actually Get to Iriomote

Iriomote's water stays diveable year-round thanks to Okinawa's subtropical climate, but conditions and critter activity shift with the seasons. Broadly:

- **March to June** is a sweet spot — settling weather, good visibility, and increasing nudibranch and juvenile-fish activity as water warms.
- **July to September** brings the warmest water (often 28–30°C) and the most stable calm-water conditions inside the harbor mouth, though this is also typhoon season for the wider Yaeyama region, so trips can get rescheduled with short notice.
- **October to November** is a quieter, comfortable shoulder season with fewer travelers and still-good muck conditions.
- **December to February** is coolest (water around 22–24°C), which calls for a 5mm wetsuit or drysuit, but muck sites like Minato remain productive since critter activity doesn't depend on warm water the way reef-fish schooling does.

Getting to Iriomote is part of the adventure. There's no airport on the island large enough for direct international connections, so the standard route is:

1. Fly into **Naha Airport** on Okinawa's main island (direct flights from Tokyo, Osaka, Fukuoka, and other major Japanese hubs, roughly 2.5–3 hours from Tokyo).
2. Connect to **Ishigaki Airport** (about 1 hour from Naha).
3. Take a high-speed ferry from **Ishigaki Port** to Iriomote — roughly 35–45 minutes to Ohara Port or 40–50 minutes to Uehara Port, depending on which side of the island your dive shop operates from.

Door to door from Tokyo, budget a full travel day: flight to Naha, connection to Ishigaki, then the ferry crossing. Most dive shops will coordinate pickup near the port your ferry lands at, since Iriomote has very limited public transport — a rental car or shop shuttle is the practical way to move around once you arrive. Minato itself is a short boat ride from the harbor, so dive shops based in Ohara have the easiest access to this specific site.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette for a Muck Dive Done Right

**Booking:** Iriomote has a small number of dive operators compared to Ishigaki or Okinawa's main island, and the good muck-diving guides book up during peak Japanese holiday windows (Golden Week in late April/early May, and the summer Obon period in mid-August). Reach out to a shop directly by email or phone at least a few weeks ahead if you're traveling in those windows; shoulder-season trips can often be arranged with just a few days' notice. Confirm your shop specifically dives Minato or similar muck sites — some operators focus almost entirely on Iriomote's outer reefs and manta cleaning stations, so it's worth asking.

**Gear:** A 3mm wetsuit covers most of the warm months; step up to 5mm or add a hood/vest in winter. Because this is a critter-hunting dive, gear priorities shift from what you'd bring to a wide-angle reef dive:
- A **fine-tipped pointer or reef stick** helps guides indicate tiny subjects without disturbing the substrate.
- A **macro lens or dedicated macro compact** gets far more use here than a wide-angle rig.
- A **primary light plus a small backup light** helps pick detail out of silty water and check inside crevices.
- Good **buoyancy control is non-negotiable** — fin kicks that stir up silt ruin visibility for the rest of the group and can bury the very critters you came to find.

**Etiquette:** Because Minato's substrate is soft sand and silt rather than hard coral, careless finning does real damage to visibility and can smother small creatures. Guides will typically ask divers to hover rather than stand or kneel on the bottom, and to avoid touching anything, including "harmless-looking" rubble that might be someone's camouflage. Photographers should resist the urge to move subjects or bait critters into better poses — respected local shops enforce a strict hands-off policy and will end a dive early for divers who don't comply.

**Where to stay and eat:** Both Ohara and Uehara have small clusters of guesthouses and pensions geared toward divers, several with in-house restaurants serving Okinawan and Yaeyama specialties like *yaeyama soba* and fresh reef fish. Book lodging near whichever port your dive shop uses, since the island's east and west sides are a long drive apart and public transport is minimal. Many guesthouses will also handle laundry and gear rinsing, which is worth asking about given how much salt and silt a few days of muck diving puts on your equipment.
