## Who Dives Here, and Why Maeda Point Earns the Hype

Blue Cave — locally known as Seiganto or Aoi Dokutsu — sits inside Maeda Point, a basalt headland on Okinawa's west coast that has quietly become one of the most photographed dive entries in Japan. The draw is simple: a half-submerged sea cave where sunlight bounces off the white sandy floor and refracts into an electric cobalt glow before it ever reaches a diver's mask. You don't need technical cave training to see it — the cavern mouth is wide, shallow, and swimmable in a single breath hold of attention, which is exactly why it has become a rite of passage for newly certified divers and snorkelers alike.

What sets this spot apart from other "blue cave" attractions around the world is that it isn't a one-trick photo op. Maeda Point is a full reef dive site in its own right, with table coral gardens, overhangs, and swim-throughs radiating out from the cave entrance. You can start a dive in the cave's blue light, spend the rest of the tank cruising a healthy fringing reef, and surface having covered two completely different kinds of underwater scenery. That combination — accessible cave, legitimate reef, shore entry, no boat required — is rare, and it's the reason local dive shops run trips here almost every day of the year regardless of swell elsewhere on the island.

This page is built for three overlapping audiences: open-water certified divers doing their first cave-adjacent dive, snorkelers who want to see the blue glow without a tank, and intermediate divers using Maeda as a warm-up or night-dive site before heading to Okinawa's more current-exposed walls further offshore. If you fall into any of those groups, keep reading — the logistics here matter more than at most sites because of how popular and tide-dependent the entry point is.

## Inside the Cave and Around the Reef — What the Dive Actually Looks Like

The cave itself sits at the base of the Maeda cliffs, reachable by a short surface swim or fin from the main shore-entry steps. Depth at the cave mouth runs 3–6 meters, shallow enough that even snorkelers can look straight down into the chamber from the surface and watch the light show. Divers typically descend just outside the entrance, drop to the sandy floor, and swim in under the rock ceiling, where the opening acts like a lens — only blue wavelengths survive the filtering effect of the water above, so the chamber floor glows an almost unnatural neon blue on sunny mornings. It's a brief, slow-motion moment rather than a long cave penetration, well within recreational no-light-required limits, though a dive light helps pick out the color contrast in the cave's darker back corners.

From there, instructors usually guide the group out along the reef shelf that wraps south and north of the point. Expect a gentle slope from 5 to 18 meters, dotted with table coral, soft coral patches, and small bommies that shelter clownfish, parrotfish, and the occasional reef shark sighting in deeper sections. Visibility on calm days regularly exceeds 15–20 meters, and the site's semi-sheltered bay geography means current is mild compared to Okinawa's outer reef passes — one reason it's a favorite for introductory dives and refresher courses. Night dives are also offered here; the cave's silhouette against torchlight is a different, equally popular experience, and nocturnal reef life (octopus, shrimp, sleeping parrotfish) is active on the surrounding coral shelf.

The one wrinkle divers should plan around is entry logistics. There's no boat ramp or dock — you walk down a set of stone/concrete steps from the clifftop parking area, carrying full gear, then make a rocky-bottom shore entry that's easiest at mid-to-high tide and genuinely awkward at low tide when exposed rock and urchins narrow the safe path. Guided trips handle this with local knowledge of the tide windows; independent divers should check tide tables before committing to a self-guided session.

## When to Go and How to Get to Maeda Point

Okinawa's dive season runs essentially year-round thanks to subtropical water temperatures (24°C in winter, up to 29–30°C in summer), but conditions at Blue Cave vary by season in ways worth planning around. April through October brings the warmest water, best cave light penetration (the sun angle matters for that signature blue glow — late morning to early afternoon is prime), and the highest crowds, since this window overlaps peak tourist season and Japanese school holidays. June to early July is also rainy season, which can cloud runoff visibility after heavy rain, though the open-ocean side of Maeda is usually less affected than inshore sites. November through March is noticeably quieter, water stays comfortably diveable in a 5mm wetsuit, and the cave light is still visible, just softer — this is the stretch locals recommend for divers who want the experience without a crowd of snorkelers queuing at the cave mouth.

Typhoon season (roughly August to October) is the one real access risk — swell and wind can shut shore entries down for a day or two at a time, so build a buffer day into any trip itinerary during those months.

Getting there: Maeda Point is on Okinawa's west coast in Onna Village, about 45–60 minutes by car or taxi from Naha Airport (OKA), Okinawa's main air hub, depending on traffic through the Naha urban area. There's no train service on Okinawa Island, so a rental car or a dive shop's included pickup van are the two realistic options; renting a car at the airport is straightforward and gives you flexibility to combine the dive with other Onna Village or Chatan-area stops. If you're staying in the Naha/Chatan resort strip, many dive operators offer hotel pickup, which removes the parking-and-navigation hassle entirely. Paid parking lots sit directly above the cave stairs, filling up fast on weekends and holidays, so arriving by shop shuttle is genuinely the easier choice if your schedule allows it.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette Before You Go Under

Nearly all diving at Blue Cave is run through local shops based in Onna Village or nearby Chatan — this isn't a site you show up to with your own tank unless you're an experienced local diver with your own gear and a dive buddy. Book a guided shore-dive package in advance, especially in peak summer months and on weekends, when both parking and shop slots fill early. Most operators offer this as a half-day trip combining the cave with one or two reef dives, and many run English-language guiding specifically because of how international the clientele here is.

Gear-wise, a 3mm wetsuit is plenty from late spring through autumn; bring or rent a 5mm for winter diving. Reef shoes or sturdy sandals are worth packing separately from your dive booties, since the walk down to the water is over uneven rock and concrete steps that are slippery when wet. If you're snorkeling rather than diving, a well-fitted mask matters more than fins here — the cave viewing is mostly about looking down through calm, clear water rather than swimming hard.

Etiquette and conservation notes locals care about: Maeda Point sees heavy daily traffic from both certified divers and casual snorkelers, so the coral around the entry point has taken visible wear — maintain careful buoyancy near the reef shelf, avoid standing on coral to rest, and don't touch or chase marine life for photos. Reef-safe sunscreen is both an environmental courtesy and, increasingly, something local shops will ask about directly. After the dive, Onna Village has a cluster of casual seafood and Okinawan soba spots within a short drive, and it's common for dive groups to debrief over a meal before heading back toward Naha — a relaxed, practical way to close out what is, for many visitors, their first real cave-and-reef dive in Japan.
