## Why Surfers Keep Coming Back to Ichinomiya

Ichinomiya Beach sits on the Kujukuri coastline in Chiba Prefecture, and it has quietly become one of the most important surf spots in Japan — important enough that it hosted the surfing events at the Tokyo 2020 Olympics. But its reputation isn't built on being a world-class heavy wave; it's built on the opposite. This is a beach that rewards consistency over spectacle, which is exactly why it has become the training ground for generations of Japanese surfers and the first stop for curious beginners from Tokyo.

The wave here is a textbook beach break: sand bottom, no reef to worry about, and swell that wraps in reliably from the Pacific almost every day of the year. Kujukuri literally means "99 ri" (an old unit of distance), referring to the roughly 60-kilometer stretch of nearly uninterrupted sandy shoreline that Ichinomiya is part of. That geography is the whole story — an unbroken, gently sloping sand coast facing directly into the open ocean, with nothing blocking incoming swell. The result is a wave that's forgiving to fall on, easy to read, and almost always doing something rideable, even when other spots in the Kanto region are flat.

What makes Ichinomiya distinct from other Chiba breaks like Isumi or Katsuura is sheer consistency and the density of surf infrastructure that's grown up around it. There are more surf schools, board rentals, and surf-specific guesthouses here per kilometer than almost anywhere else in Japan. It's less a hidden gem and more an institution — the Waikiki of Japan, if Waikiki were an hour and a half from a megacity of 14 million people. For a first-time surfer, a traveling intermediate wanting to log easy wave count, or a family looking for a safe beach activity with a surf lesson attached, Ichinomiya is the default answer in the Kanto region, and for good reason.

## Reading the Wave: Swell, Sandbars, and Beach Layout

Ichinomiya's wave is produced almost entirely by the open-ocean swell window of the Pacific, since there's no significant reef, point, or headland shaping it — just sand. That means a few practical things for anyone planning a session:

**Wave character.** Expect soft-to-moderate beach-break peaks, mostly A-frames and shifting left/right shoulders depending on where the sandbars have built up that week. Sandbars here move with the tide and swell direction, so the exact best peak shifts along the beach from month to month. Locals and the surf schools track this constantly — if you're taking a lesson, the instructor's local knowledge of where the sandbar is sitting that day is worth more than any forecast.

**Swell and wind.** The coast faces roughly east, so it picks up swell generated by typhoons passing offshore to the south and east, as well as more general North Pacific swell. The cleanest conditions tend to happen with light offshore (westerly, from land) or calm wind in the early morning, before the typical afternoon onshore sea breeze picks up and turns the face choppy. This is a universal pattern on this coast — plan sessions for dawn to mid-morning if you want glass.

**Size and crowd.** Wave height is typically in the knee-to-chest range for a big chunk of the year, occasionally building to head-high or better when typhoon swell tracks closer. Because it's so beginner-accessible, the inside section near the schools can get crowded on weekends and holidays with lesson groups; if you're more experienced and want space, paddle further down the beach away from the main school clusters or go at first light.

**Bottom and hazards.** Pure sand bottom, no rocks or reef, which is a big part of why this is considered one of the safest breaks in Japan to learn on. The main hazards are rip currents that form around the shifting sandbars (ask your instructor or the lifeguard station where the current is running that day) and, in summer, jellyfish and the occasional sharp increase in crowd density. There are lifeguard-patrolled sections (uminoie) in peak season — stick close to them if you're a beginner or unfamiliar with the beach.

## Season and Access: When to Go and How to Get There

**Best season.** Ichinomiya is a genuine year-round surf spot, which is rare for Japan. Broadly:

- **Spring (March–May):** Smaller, friendlier swell, mild water, good for beginners and lesson bookings pick up as the weather warms.
- **Summer (June–August):** Warmest water, busiest beach (swimming season overlaps with surf season), typhoon swell starting to arrive from July onward brings bigger, more consistent waves, but also crowds and heat.
- **Autumn (September–November):** Arguably the best window — typhoon swell is most active, water is still warm from summer, and the crowds thin out after the summer holidays end. Early-to-mid autumn (like now) is a sweet spot for size and water temperature.
- **Winter (December–February):** Smaller average swell and cold water (a good wetsuit, boots, and possibly gloves become necessary), but far fewer people and still rideable surf on most days for those properly geared up.

**Getting there from Tokyo.** Ichinomiya is one of the most accessible surf towns in the country for anyone based in central Tokyo, which is a huge part of its appeal:

- **By train:** Take the JR Sobu or Keiyo line route to Chiba, then transfer to the JR Tokyo Bay-Aqua Line or more directly the JR Gaiku/Sotobo Line toward Ichinomiya Station. A more common routing is the JR Limited Express "Wakashio" direct from Tokyo Station to Ichinomiya Station, which takes around 75–90 minutes. From Ichinomiya Station, the beach is about a 10–15 minute taxi ride or a flat, bikeable 3–4 km — many guesthouses rent bicycles or offer station pickup.
- **By car:** From central Tokyo, take the Keiyo Road/Ken-O Expressway and continue onto Route 128 toward Ichinomiya; driving time is typically around 90 minutes to 2 hours depending on traffic, which can be heavy on summer weekends heading to the coast.
- **By air:** If coming from further afield, Narita International Airport is actually closer to Ichinomiya than central Tokyo is — roughly 45–60 minutes by car or limousine bus, making this a very practical first or last stop on a Japan trip for traveling surfers with an international flight to catch.

This proximity to both a major international airport and Japan's largest city is a big part of why Ichinomiya built up such a dense surf economy — it's genuinely a viable day trip from Tokyo, not just a dedicated surf-travel destination.

## Practical Tips: Booking, Gear, Etiquette, and Where to Stay

**Lessons and rentals.** Because Ichinomiya is the default beginner destination for the Kanto region, there are dozens of surf schools clustered around the beach, many offering English-friendly instruction given the Olympic exposure. Board and wetsuit rental is widely available and inexpensive by Japanese standards; most shops are set up for walk-ins but weekend slots in summer and early autumn fill up, so booking a day or two ahead is worth it if you have a fixed schedule.

**What to bring.** A rash guard or light wetsuit top is useful even in summer for paddle-chafe protection; from late autumn through spring, a full 3/2mm or thicker wetsuit plus boots is recommended since Chiba water temperatures drop noticeably in winter. Reef shoes aren't necessary given the sand bottom, but they help with the sometimes-hot sand walk to the water in midsummer.

**Etiquette.** Lesson groups occupy a lot of the inside, beginner-friendly section, so if you're a more experienced surfer, give them generous space rather than snaking the easy inside peaks — it's both safer and considered polite. Standard right-of-way rules apply (the surfer closest to the peak has priority), and Japanese surf culture places real value on a friendly nod or "ohayou gozaimasu" (good morning) in the water, especially at a spot this community-oriented.

**Food and stay.** The town of Ichinomiya has built an entire micro-economy around surf travel: surf-themed cafes, casual seafood restaurants, and a range of guesthouses and surf lodges within walking distance of the sand, many run by surfers themselves and happy to give real-time wave reports. Staying overnight rather than day-tripping is worth considering if you want the classic dawn session before the wind turns onshore — most lesson groups and casual visitors don't show up until mid-morning, so early birds get the cleanest, least crowded water.

**Combine it with other interests.** Chōsei District and the wider Kujukuri coastline are flat and easy to explore by bicycle, and the area is also known for small local shrines, fresh seafood markets, and a laid-back beach-town pace that's a deliberate contrast to central Tokyo — worth a slower day or two even for non-surfers tagging along.
