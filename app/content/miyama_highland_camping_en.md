## Why Miyama Highland Stands Out From Other Kyoto Campgrounds

Most campers heading out of Kyoto City end up at one of the crowded lakeside or riverside sites closer to the urban sprawl. Miyama Highland Camping Park is different — it sits in the hills above the Miyama district, a part of northern Kyoto Prefecture famous for its cluster of kayabuki-buki (thatched-roof) farmhouses, some of which have stood for over 200 years. You get the forest-camp experience — pine and cedar canopy, cool highland air, genuinely dark skies for stargazing — combined with easy access to a living rural village that still functions as a community, not a museum reconstruction.

This makes the park a strong fit for a specific kind of camper: people who want quiet, elevation, and tree cover, but who also want something to do beyond the campsite itself. Families can pitch a tent in the morning and walk the thatched village in the afternoon. Photographers get golden-hour light on both the forest ridge and the farmhouse rooftops without moving the car more than fifteen minutes. Solo campers looking to disconnect will find the site far enough from Kyoto's tourist corridors that mobile signal thins out and the noise floor drops to birdsong and wind in the cedars.

It's also a legitimate mountain camp in the technical sense — the park sits at meaningful elevation on the highland plateau, which means noticeably cooler nights than Kyoto City even in mid-summer, and a real chance of frost in the shoulder seasons. That's a feature for anyone trying to escape the basin heat that makes Kyoto notoriously humid and stifling from July through August, but it does mean packing differently than you would for a beach or riverside site.

## Camp Layout, Terrain, and What the Grounds Actually Feel Like

The park is laid out across a gently sloped forest clearing, with individual tent sites tucked among mature trees rather than arranged on an open lawn. That layout has practical consequences: expect a mix of sun and shade throughout the day, root systems and uneven ground in some pitches (bring a footprint and stakes rated for compact soil), and enough natural separation between sites that neighboring groups rarely feel close. This is not a manicured resort-style campground — it reads as a genuine forest clearing that's been developed just enough to be safe and comfortable.

Facilities are geared toward car camping and cabin stays rather than backcountry minimalism. There are managed restroom and wash blocks, and many highland camps in this region also offer log cabins or bungalows as an alternative to tents — useful if you're traveling with young children or want a hedge against a cold snap. Communal fire pits or designated bonfire areas are typical at these mountain sites, though rules on cutting or gathering wood are usually strict, so plan to buy firewood on-site or bring your own from an approved source rather than foraging in the surrounding forest.

Because the terrain is forested and sloped rather than flat and open, this isn't the easiest first-time camp for someone who has never pitched a tent outside a flat backyard. Give yourself extra daylight on setup day, choose a site with visibly level ground, and orient your tent door away from the prevailing downhill wind that channels through highland valleys in the evening. If you're bringing a larger family tent or awning, walk the site before committing — canopy cover varies a lot from one pitch to the next, and a spot that looks shady at noon can be in direct afternoon sun by 3pm depending on which side of the ridge it's on.

## When to Visit and How to Get There From Kyoto Station

**Best season:** Late spring through early autumn (May to October) is the core camping window, with early June and September/October standing out as the sweet spots — cooler nights, fewer insects than peak summer, and in autumn, the added bonus of the Miyama valley's foliage turning color a few weeks ahead of central Kyoto. Midsummer (late July–August) is workable thanks to the elevation keeping temperatures several degrees below the city, but it's also peak domestic travel season, so book well ahead. Winter closes most tent camping here — snow is common on the highland plateau, and many facilities shut or scale down from December through March, though the thatched village itself becomes a well-known snow-viewing destination if you're visiting without camping gear.

**Getting there from Kyoto Station:** A car is the practical option. The drive from Kyoto Station to the Miyama area takes roughly 60–75 minutes via a combination of the Keihoku road and local mountain routes, depending on traffic and exact site location — rental cars are widely available at Kyoto Station and Kyoto International Airport transfer points. If you're driving, note that the final stretch into the highland camp area involves narrow, winding mountain roads; drive them in daylight the first time, especially if towing gear or unfamiliar with switchbacks.

**Without a car:** It's possible but slower. Take a JR or private rail connection toward Sonobe or Hiyoshi Station, then transfer to one of the local community buses that run into the Miyama/Chii village area — schedules are infrequent (sometimes just a handful of runs per day), so check current timetables before relying on this route, and factor in a taxi or short walk for the final leg to the campground itself. Given the bus frequency, most non-drivers either join a tour, rent a car for the day, or coordinate a pickup with their accommodation.

**Nearest airport reference:** Kansai International Airport (KIX) is roughly 2 to 2.5 hours away by car or a combination of train and car, making this a realistic day-two stop after landing and overnighting in Kyoto City rather than a direct-from-airport destination.

## Booking, Packing, and Etiquette for a Smooth Stay

**Book ahead, especially for weekends and fall foliage season.** Miyama's popularity as a thatched-village destination means camping and cabin availability tightens fast around autumn color season and Japanese national holidays (Golden Week in early May, Obon in mid-August). Reserve directly through the park's booking channel or by phone where English support may be limited — having dates, party size, and tent-vs-cabin preference ready in a simple written note helps if you're calling in.

**Pack for real temperature swings.** Because this is a mountain camp, nights can run 10°C or more cooler than Kyoto City even in summer, and shoulder-season mornings can dip close to freezing. Bring a sleeping bag rated colder than you think you'll need, a warm layer for after sunset, and a groundmat — cold transfers up through forest soil faster than most campers expect.

**Firewood and fire etiquette:** Don't gather deadwood or branches from the surrounding forest; buy bundled firewood on-site or from a local shop, and keep any fire contained to designated pits. Highland forests dry out in late summer and early autumn, and campground rules on open flame tend to be enforced strictly during dry spells.

**Respect the working village nearby.** The thatched-roof settlement isn't a theme park — people live and farm there year-round. Stick to marked paths and viewing areas, be discreet photographing private homes, and support the local economy by eating at village restaurants or buying from small shops rather than just passing through with a camera.

**Combine the trip with village sightseeing.** Most visitors treat the campground as a base for exploring the Miyama thatched village, nearby hiking trails, and the Yura River valley. Set aside a half-day for the village walk, and check with the campground or local tourist information for seasonal events — the village's illuminated snow-roof nights in winter and the surrounding hills' autumn colors are both well known regionally, even though they fall outside the main camping season.

**Wildlife and food storage:** As with most forested Japanese mountain camps, secure food overnight — raccoon dogs (tanuki) and other small wildlife are common visitors to unattended coolers. Basic food discipline (sealed containers, nothing left out overnight) keeps both your supplies and the local fauna undisturbed.
