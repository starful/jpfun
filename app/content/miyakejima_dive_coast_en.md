## Why Divers Keep Coming Back to Miyakejima

Miyakejima sits in the Izu island chain, technically part of Tokyo Prefecture even though it feels like a different country entirely. The island is an active volcano — Mount Oyama last erupted in 2000, and the eruption reshaped not just the landscape above water but the reefs below it. That history is exactly what makes the diving here distinct: instead of soft coral gardens, you get basalt canyons, lava tubes, black sand slopes, and walls pocked with volcanic bubbles that no typhoon or reef-building coral could ever produce naturally. Visibility regularly sits at 15-25 meters thanks to the Kuroshio Current sweeping warm, clean water past the island, so the drama of the terrain is rarely hidden behind murk.

This is not a beginner-only destination, but it isn't exclusively technical either. Miyakejima works well for intermediate divers who have logged a few dozen dives and want current-aware sites with genuine topography, and it also rewards experienced divers chasing hammerhead encounters, dolphin pods, and macro life tucked into volcanic crevices. What sets it apart from the more famous Izu Oshima or Izu Peninsula shore-dive scene is the sheer variety packed into one small island: you can dive a gentle black-sand bay in the morning and a current-swept basalt wall in the afternoon, often changing conditions entirely by just driving to the other side of the island. Local dive shops know the coastline intimately and adjust sites daily based on wind direction, which is a point of pride on an island where the sea can flip from glassy to rough within hours.

## Dive Site Conditions and What to Expect Underwater

Miyakejima's dive sites are scattered around the island's coastline, and shops choose sites day-of based on wind and swell rather than a fixed rotation, so flexibility is part of the culture here.

- **Okubo Hama** — A sheltered, shallow entry point with black volcanic sand, often used for checkout dives and night dives. Calm most of the year, making it the fallback site when swell picks up elsewhere.
- **Sabigahama** — Famous for schools of pennant fish practically carpeting the shallows, plus occasional turtle sightings. Easy shore access and gentle slope, good for photography-focused dives.
- **Izu (Miyake's own Izu Point, not to be confused with the Izu Peninsula)** — A rockier, current-influenced site where larger pelagics pass through; divers with drift experience get the most out of it.
- **Tairo** — Known for dramatic lava formations and small swim-throughs; visibility here tends to be the clearest on the island on good days.
- **Point sites off the harbor breakwaters** — Used when wind makes the open coast unworkable; still interesting thanks to the volcanic rubble attracting juvenile fish and nudibranchs.

Water temperature swings meaningfully with season: around 18-20°C in winter (a 7mm wetsuit or drysuit territory) and up to 26-28°C in late summer, when a 3-5mm suit is comfortable for most divers. Because the island sits in open ocean rather than a sheltered bay, swell and current are the main variables shops manage — guides routinely dive one coast in the morning and switch to the opposite coast in the afternoon as wind direction shifts, so trust local judgment over any fixed site list you find online. Hammerhead shark sightings cluster in early summer, while dolphin encounters (a mix of resident and visiting pods) can happen at almost any time of year, though never guaranteed.

## When to Go and How to Get There From Tokyo

Diving is possible year-round, but conditions and marine life shift by season:

- **Late spring to early summer (May-June)** — Water starts warming, visibility improves, and this is prime hammerhead shark season.
- **Summer (July-September)** — Warmest water, most stable weather, but also typhoon season, which can cancel ferries and flights with little notice.
- **Autumn (October-November)** — Fewer crowds, water still comfortable, generally stable seas before winter swell builds. A strong window for divers who want calmer logistics.
- **Winter (December-March)** — Coldest water and roughest seas, but the clearest visibility of the year and a good chance of dolphin encounters for drysuit-equipped divers.

Getting to Miyakejima is part of the adventure. From central Tokyo, the most common route is the overnight ferry from Takeshiba Pier, operated by Tokai Kisen, which departs late evening and arrives early the next morning after roughly 6.5-7 hours — many divers book a reserved second-class cabin to actually sleep. The faster option is a flight from Chofu Airport in western Tokyo, which takes about 50 minutes and is operated by New Central Airlines on small turboprop aircraft; seats are limited and flights are occasionally cancelled in high wind, so build in a buffer day if your schedule is tight. There is no bullet train or direct expressway option since Miyakejima is an island — ferry or small aircraft are the only ways in. Once on the island, a rental car is strongly recommended since dive shops and sites are spread around the coastline and public transport is sparse; most shops also offer pickup from the port or airport if you book ahead.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette for a Smooth Trip

Book your dive shop before you book transport, not after. Because flights are small and ferries sail only once a day, popular shops fill up fast in peak season (July-August and the hammerhead window in May-June), and some shops require advance notice to arrange guided boat or shore dives around that day's wind conditions. Most shops rent full gear sets, but divers with their own wetsuit and regulator should bring them — rental drysuits in winter can be limited in stock, so reserve early if diving December through March.

A few practical notes that make the trip smoother:

- **Confirm cancellation policies before booking ferry or flights.** Typhoon season and general island weather can disrupt both, and dive shops are used to rescheduling — ask what their policy is if your transport gets cancelled.
- **Bring cash.** Card acceptance on the island is inconsistent outside larger lodging, and smaller dive shops and local eateries often prefer cash.
- **Respect the volcanic terrain.** Lava rock formations are sharp and can be fragile in places; avoid touching or standing on formations near swim-throughs both for your safety and to prevent breaking delicate structures.
- **Check volcanic gas advisories.** As an active volcano island, parts of Miyakejima occasionally post gas advisories for hiking areas; this rarely affects diving but is worth a quick check if you plan to combine diving with a hike up Mount Oyama.
- **Stay options are simple but comfortable.** Minshuku (family-run guesthouses) dominate accommodation, many catering specifically to divers with gear-drying areas and early breakfasts timed for morning boat departures. Booking directly with a minshuku tied to your chosen dive shop often streamlines pickup logistics.
- **Food leans toward fresh seafood and simple home cooking.** Flying fish (tobiuo) dishes are a local specialty worth trying after a day underwater, and most minshuku serve dinner included in the room rate.
- **Pack for self-sufficiency.** The island has limited convenience stores and no large supermarkets, so bring any specific snacks, medications, or dive accessories you can't easily replace locally.

Miyakejima rewards divers willing to deal with a slightly more complicated journey than Okinawa or the Izu Peninsula shore dives closer to central Honshu. In exchange, you get volcanic terrain, strong visibility, and a quieter, more local diving culture — all technically still within Tokyo.
