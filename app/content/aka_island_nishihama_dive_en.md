## Why Nishihama Belongs on Every Kerama Diver's List

Aka Island doesn't get the name recognition of Zamami next door, and that is exactly why divers who find it tend to come back. Nishihama Beach sits on the island's southwest shore, a crescent of pale sand that most visitors photograph for the color of the water alone — a gradient running from turquoise shallows to a deep cobalt channel within a few hundred meters of the shoreline. What they don't always realize is that the same water is home to some of the most intact coral cover left in the Kerama Islands National Park, a protected marine zone that has kept boat traffic, anchoring, and runoff under tighter control than almost anywhere else in Okinawa.

This spot works for a specific kind of diver. If you want a liveaboard-grade reef without a liveaboard-grade commitment, Nishihama delivers it from a beach entry. If you're newly certified and nervous about current, the inner reef terrace is sheltered and shallow enough to build confidence before a boat takes you to Kerama's more exposed channels. And if you're a seasoned diver chasing hawksbill and green turtles, Nishihama's resident population is reliable enough that local shops will quietly tell you which coral heads to check first. What it is not is a site for people who want nightlife, a dive resort with a pool bar, or fast turnaround between tanks — Aka is a small, slow island, and the dive culture here matches that pace. Divers looking for big-animal pelagic action (mantas, hammerheads) should look toward Yonaguni or the Kerama channel dives instead; Nishihama's appeal is density and clarity of reef life in a compact, repeatable site rather than open-water drama.

## Reading the Reef: Dive Sites, Depths, and What Actually Lives There

Nishihama is really two dives depending on how far you swim from the beach. The inner shelf runs from the shoreline out to roughly 8–12 meters, a mix of staghorn and table coral interspersed with sand channels, and it's where almost every shore-dive briefing starts. Visibility here can be reduced right after rain, since it's the shallowest and closest to the small river mouth north of the beach, but on a clear day it still runs 15–20 meters.

Beyond the inner shelf, the reef drops in a series of steps to a sandy plateau around 18–22 meters, and this is where the site earns its reputation. Table coral colonies here are genuinely large — some easily three meters across — and largely undamaged by the 1998 and 2016 bleaching events that hit shallower Okinawan reefs harder. Expect to see hawksbill turtles resting under overhangs, schools of convict tang and sergeant major moving over the coral heads, and if you're diving early morning or late afternoon, reef sharks are an occasional but not rare sighting in the deeper sand channels. Macro life is strong too: nudibranchs, mantis shrimp, and juvenile fish hide in the rubble zones between coral stands, which is why many local shops bring guests back for a second dive focused purely on critter-spotting rather than reef touring.

Current at Nishihama is generally mild compared to Kerama's channel dives between islands, which makes it a genuine beginner-to-intermediate site — but it is still open ocean, and an incoming tide can create a noticeable pull along the outer edge of the plateau. Dive shops on Aka run this as both a shore dive and a short boat hop, and most will adjust the plan on the day based on swell direction; a south wind pushes chop straight into the bay, while north or west winds leave it glassy.

## Season, Weather Windows, and Getting to Aka From Naha

Diving at Nishihama is possible nearly year-round, but the comfortable window runs from April through November, with water temperatures ranging from around 23°C in early spring to a warm 29–30°C across July and August. May and June bring the clearest average visibility of the year, before the summer typhoon season adds unpredictability between late July and September — not a reason to avoid those months outright, but worth building a flexible itinerary around, since ferries to the Kerama Islands are the first service suspended when a typhoon approaches. Winter diving (December–March) is quieter, cooler, and often has the best visibility of all on calm days, but a 5mm wetsuit or hooded vest becomes a good idea, and ferry schedules thin out.

Access starts in Naha. From Tomari Port (also called Tomarin), the Kerama Kisen ferry company runs both a high-speed boat (Marine Liner, around 50 minutes to Aka) and a slower car ferry (Kerama, around 90 minutes to Zamami, with onward connection or a separate direct Aka service depending on the season's timetable). Naha Airport to Tomari Port is roughly a 15-minute taxi ride or a short bus/monorail-plus-walk combination; budget an hour from touchdown to boarding to be safe, since ferry counters close check-in around 10 minutes before departure. If you're staying in central Naha the night before, Tomari Port is walkable from several budget guesthouses near Kokusai-dori.

There is no rental car on Aka worth mentioning for most visitors — the island is small enough to cover on foot, by the free-ish shared minivan many guesthouses run, or by rental bicycle, and Nishihama Beach itself is a 10–15 minute walk from the port and the main cluster of guesthouses and dive shops. Day-trippers from Naha are possible (first ferry out, last ferry back) but tight if you want two tanks plus a surface interval and lunch; staying one night on Aka turns this from a rushed errand into an actual dive trip.

## Booking, Gear, and Reef Etiquette Before You Head In

Book your dive shop before you book the ferry, not after — Aka has only a handful of operators, and summer weekends fill fast, especially for the 8am boat that times the best visibility window. Most shops offer a shore-dive package specifically built around Nishihama for certified divers (bring your C-card and logbook) and a shallow discover-scuba session for anyone diving for the first time. If you're traveling with your own gear, a 3mm full suit covers summer, a 5mm or semi-dry suit covers winter, and reef shoes are genuinely useful for the entry point, which has a rocky patch before the sand.

Reef-safe sunscreen is not a suggestion here — the Kerama Islands National Park designation means local operators actively check for it, and oxybenzone-based sunscreens are effectively unwelcome given how directly they affect the coral this site is known for. Buoyancy control matters more at Nishihama than at sites with less coral cover: table coral structures are fragile at the edges even though they look solid, and a stray fin kick can do damage that took decades to grow. Stay off the sand directly beneath overhangs where resting turtles tend to be, and never chase or corner one for a photo — they'll surface for air on their own schedule, and crowding them is both poor etiquette and, in a national park, technically against the rules.

For food and stay, Aka's guesthouses are small and mostly family-run, several offering dinner that includes the day's catch — ask what's fresh rather than ordering off a fixed menu. Cash is still the safer assumption for guesthouses and smaller dive shops; card readers exist but connectivity on Aka can be patchy enough that a backup in cash avoids an awkward moment at checkout. Pack light, bring a dry bag for the ferry crossing, and build in a buffer day if you're visiting during typhoon season — Kerama's beauty is largely a function of weather nobody controls, and the islands reward travelers who don't fight the schedule.
