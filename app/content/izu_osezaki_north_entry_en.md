## Who This Spot Is For

Osezaki's North Entry is not the place to go if you want big animals, blue water, or a dramatic wall. It is the place to go if you love staring at a patch of volcanic sand for forty minutes until a juvenile frogfish, a flamboyant cuttlefish, or a tiny octopus reveals itself. This is muck diving in its purest Japanese form: a gently sloping black-sand shore, scattered debris and rubble patches, old pilings, and enough nutrient-rich runoff from Suruga Bay to support a genuinely absurd density of small, strange, and photogenic critters.

The North Entry is the quieter, less crowded sibling of Osezaki's main (Ippongi) entry point further south on the peninsula. Divers who already know Osezaki tend to split their day between the two — a wide-angle or drift-style dive at the main point, then a slow macro crawl at the North Entry where currents are gentler and the sand is softer underfoot. If you are a photographer with a 100mm macro lens and the patience of a heron, this is your spot. If you are chasing manta rays or big pelagics, keep driving south toward the Izu outer coast instead.

It also suits divers who want low-stress logistics. There is no boat, no tide-dependent window that forces an early wake-up, and no long surface swim before the fun starts. You kit up at the car, walk a short distance to the water, and you are on the bottom within a few minutes. For visiting divers without a car-mounted tank rack or a local dive shop membership, that simplicity is a real selling point.

## Dive Conditions and What You'll See

The North Entry sits on the western, Suruga Bay-facing side of the Osezaki peninsula, tucked into Numazu's Izu coastline. The bottom is a mix of fine black volcanic sand and patches of rubble, broken shell, and the occasional discarded rope or tire — classic muck-diving terrain where critters use debris as camouflage and shelter. Depth at this entry is forgiving: most of the interesting macro life sits between 5 and 15 meters, with the sandy slope easing out gradually rather than dropping off sharply. This makes it a comfortable site for divers working on buoyancy control or doing long, slow critter-hunting dives on a single tank.

Visibility is the trade-off. Expect 5 to 10 meters on an average day, sometimes less after rain pushes runoff from the surrounding hills into the bay, and occasionally better in winter when the water clears and cools. This is not a site you dive for sweeping scenery — you dive it with your mask six inches from the sand, moving in slow motion.

What divers typically find: harlequin and ornate ghost pipefish hiding in current-swept weed patches, juvenile frogfish in a rotating cast of colors, nudibranchs in genuinely startling variety year-round, flamboyant cuttlefish hunting across the open sand at dusk, mimic and wonderpus-type octopus sightings on good days, robust ghost pipefish, various species of shrimp and crab tucked into shells and bottles, and seasonal juveniles — tiny fish riding in on the Kuroshio Current that pulses warm water past this stretch of coast. Seahorses and pipefish show up in the seagrass and weed margins along the slope. Because the substrate changes with storms and seasonal sand movement, no two visits look quite the same, which keeps repeat divers coming back.

Water temperature swings meaningfully with the season — think low-to-mid 20s°C in summer and down into the mid-teens in winter — so this is a year-round site but not a year-round wetsuit.

## Season and Access from Tokyo or Numazu

Osezaki is diveable in every season, and the North Entry's macro focus actually makes winter a strong option, since the critter density and visibility both tend to improve once the summer plankton bloom clears out. That said, most visiting divers plan around these windows:

- **Spring (March–May):** Water warming, visibility improving, good macro activity as juveniles start appearing.
- **Summer (June–August):** Warmest water, busiest with local club dives and snorkelers, higher boat and surface traffic in the bay — the North Entry stays calmer than the main point even in peak season.
- **Autumn (September–November):** Often the sweet spot — warm water lingering, fewer crowds, strong macro variety as the Kuroshio pushes tropical juveniles into the bay.
- **Winter (December–February):** Cold but clear, flamboyant cuttlefish and seasonal nudibranch blooms are a highlight, and you'll likely have the entry almost to yourself.

**Getting there:** Osezaki sits on the Izu Peninsula in Numazu City, Shizuoka Prefecture, on the western shore facing Suruga Bay with Mount Fuji visible across the water on a clear day.

- **By car from Tokyo:** Roughly 2 to 2.5 hours via the Tomei Expressway to the Numazu or Nagaizumi-Numazu interchange, then local roads out to the Osezaki peninsula. A car is strongly recommended — it lets you park right at the entry, store gear, and move between the North Entry and the main Ippongi point without hauling tanks on foot.
- **By train:** Take the JR Tokaido Shinkansen or JR Tokaido Line to Numazu Station (roughly 1 hour from Tokyo on the Shinkansen), then a local bus or taxi onward to the Osezaki area — the bus connection is infrequent, so a taxi or a pre-arranged dive shop pickup is the more reliable option if you're not driving.
- **From Central Tokyo door-to-door:** Budget around 2.5 to 3 hours each way including the last-mile connection, making this a realistic long day trip, though an overnight in Numazu or on the peninsula takes the pressure off and lets you fit in a second day of diving.
- Most visiting divers book through a local Osezaki-area dive shop that handles tank fills, weights, and parking arrangements — independent shore access is possible but having a local shop sort logistics is worth it for a first visit.

## Practical Tips for Diving the North Entry

**Booking and shop logistics:** Several small dive operations are based around Osezaki and can arrange tanks, weights, and guided macro dives at the North Entry specifically — worth requesting by name, since some shops default newcomers to the busier main point. Booking a day or two ahead is usually enough outside of peak summer weekends; for Golden Week or August weekends, book further out.

**Gear:** A 5mm wetsuit or drysuit is sensible outside of peak summer — water temperature drops faster here than the air temperature suggests, especially once you're hovering motionless over sand for half an hour. A macro lens (90–105mm equivalent) with a focus light or snoot is the standard photographer's setup; a blunt muck stick or pointer for steadying yourself over sand (never on anything living) is considered good practice. Bring your own gloves if you're sensitive to cold, since this is a slow-moving dive style with a lot of hovering.

**Entry technique and etiquette:** The shore entry is a mix of pebble and sand — reef boots or sturdy fins with a heel strap help. Take your time on entry and exit; the bottom can be slippery with algae near the surf line. Because the critter population depends on an undisturbed sand and debris habitat, avoid stirring up silt with careless fin kicks (frog kick or modified flutter with a high leg position is the local norm), and never relocate "found" critters for a better photo — word travels fast in the Osezaki dive community and local shops actively discourage it.

**Buddy and dive planning:** Because visibility is modest and much of the dive is spent nose-down in the sand, buddy teams can drift apart without noticing. Agree on a turn-around air limit and a reunion plan before descending, and consider a line or reel if you plan to range far along the slope.

**Food and stay:** Numazu is well known for fresh seafood — the Numazu fish market area near the station has excellent, affordable sushi and donburi, a good reward after a cold macro dive. For an overnight, Numazu City or the nearby Izu coast towns (Shuzenji's onsen district is a scenic detour) offer a range of business hotels and traditional ryokan, letting you turn a single dive trip into a relaxed two-day Izu getaway with a soak in a hot spring to finish off a day of sand and critters.
