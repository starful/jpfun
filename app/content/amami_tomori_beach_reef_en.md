## Why Tomori Beach Is Amami's Easiest Reef Dive

Amami Oshima has no shortage of dramatic drop-offs and current-swept channels, but Tomori Beach Reef is the island's answer for divers who want coral without the drama. The reef sits just offshore from a gently sloping sand beach, so there's no boat, no long surface swim, and no technical entry to worry about. You walk in, fin out a short distance, and the reef rises up to meet you in water shallow enough that sunlight reaches every coral head.

This makes Tomori the default recommendation for newly certified divers, families diving together, and anyone doing a refresher after time away from the sport. Visibility isn't always the turquoise postcard shot you'll see from Amami's outer reefs, but the tradeoff is a forgiving, low-stress site where instructors can actually teach rather than manage a difficult entry. It's also a genuinely good macro and coral-watching site in its own right — the shallow table corals and staghorn clusters here are healthy, and the protected bay keeps them relatively undisturbed by heavy surf.

What sets it apart from Amami's more famous dive points isn't trophy marine life — you're not coming here for mantas or big pelagics — it's the combination of ease and quality. Plenty of shore dives are easy because the reef is mediocre; Tomori is easy and the reef is still worth photographing.

## What's Under the Water: Reef Layout and What You'll See

The reef structure at Tomori follows a simple, predictable profile that makes it easy to brief and easy to navigate, even for divers on their first few open-water dives after certification.

- **Entry and shallows (0–3m):** A sandy shore entry leads into scattered coral bommies within the first few fin kicks. This zone is ideal for buoyancy checks, mask-clearing practice, and getting comfortable before heading further out.
- **The reef flat (3–6m):** This is the heart of the dive — a mosaic of table coral, branching staghorn, and soft coral patches interspersed with sand channels. Depth stays forgiving throughout, which means long bottom times and minimal decompression math to think about.
- **Reef edge (6–10m):** Where the flat gives way to a gentle slope, you'll find larger coral heads and more structure for reef fish to shelter in — this is usually where the most activity concentrates.

Expect to see clownfish and anemones, parrotfish grazing on coral, wrasse, damselfish, the occasional small ray resting on sand patches, and — depending on season — juvenile reef fish using the shallows as a nursery. It's a classic subtropical coral-garden dive: not about one headline species, but about the density and health of the reef itself. Because the whole site is shallow, air consumption is low and dives commonly run 50–60 minutes, which is a real advantage for beginners who burn through tanks quickly.

Currents are minimal most of the year thanks to the bay's natural shelter, though wind direction can stir up visibility close to shore after a blow. Local dive shops check conditions daily and will simply move a session's timing or entry point rather than cancel, so it's worth being flexible by an hour or two if you book directly.

## Season and Access: When to Dive and How to Get There

**Best season:** Amami's dive season effectively runs **April through November**, with peak comfort from **May to October** when water temperatures sit in the high 24–29°C range and a 3mm wetsuit (or even a rash guard) is enough. July and August bring the warmest water and the most consistent calm conditions, but also the most sun and heat on the surface interval — bring reef-safe sunscreen and a hat. Winter diving (December–March) is possible with a thicker wetsuit, but conditions are more weather-dependent and fewer shops run daily trips.

**Typhoon awareness:** Amami sits squarely in the typhoon belt, and August–October is peak season for storms passing through the Ryukyu chain. A direct typhoon hit will shut down diving entirely for a few days; even a distant system can kick up swell that ruins visibility at exposed sites. Tomori's sheltered bay location makes it one of the first sites to reopen after a storm passes, which is another reason shops lean on it when conditions elsewhere are marginal.

**Getting there:**
- **By air:** Amami Airport (ASJ) connects to Kagoshima and Tokyo (Haneda), with flight times of roughly 1 hour from Kagoshima and around 2.5 hours from Tokyo. This is by far the fastest route onto the island.
- **By ferry:** Overnight ferries run from Kagoshima port, taking around 11 hours — a budget option if you're not in a hurry or want to bring a lot of gear.
- **From the airport to the beach:** Tomori Beach is roughly a 30–40 minute drive from Amami Airport, depending on which part of the island your accommodation is in. A rental car is the most practical option since bus service on Amami is infrequent and not built around dive schedules; most visiting divers rent a car for the duration of their stay.
- **Local hub:** Naze, the island's main town, is a useful base with the widest choice of guesthouses, dive shops, and restaurants, typically 20–30 minutes from Tomori by car.

## Practical Tips: Booking, Gear, and Etiquette

**Booking:** Dive shops on Amami are small, often one or two boats and a handful of instructors, so book at least a few days ahead in peak season (July–August) and even further out around Golden Week (late April–early May) when domestic travelers flood the island. Shore dives like Tomori are usually priced lower than boat dives — a reasonable starting budget is around ¥8,000–¥12,000 for a two-tank shore session including gear rental, though confirm current rates directly with the shop since they vary by season and group size.

**Gear:** Full rental sets (BCD, regulator, wetsuit, mask, fins) are widely available, so you don't need to fly with your own kit unless you have fit preferences. A 3mm wetsuit covers most of the warm season; bring your own if you're picky about fit, since rental sizing on a small island can be limited. Reef-safe sunscreen is worth packing — Amami's shops are increasingly strict about it given how much of the local economy depends on healthy coral.

**Reef etiquette:** The coral at Tomori is shallow enough to touch by accident, which is exactly how reefs get damaged. Keep buoyancy tight in the 3–6m zone, avoid standing on the bottom even in waist-deep water near the entry, and resist the urge to grab coral for stability — use a sand patch or your fins instead. Shops here are generally strict about no-touch policies, and for good reason: this reef's accessibility is also its vulnerability.

**Food and stay:** Naze has the best concentration of izakayas and guesthouses, with Amami's own black sugar shochu worth trying after a day of diving. If you're staying closer to Tomori itself, expect smaller, family-run minshuku rather than large hotels — book these early in peak months, as room counts are limited across the whole island. Fresh keihan (Amami's signature chicken-and-rice dish) is widely available and makes an excellent post-dive meal while you log your dive and plan the next one.
