The Tone River carves one of Gunma's deepest, greenest valleys, and Tone Valley Campground sits right where the water slows into calm, stone-lined pools before picking up speed again downstream. This is a car camp built for people who want the comfort of driving straight to their pitch — cooler, chairs, firewood, and all — without giving up the feeling of being genuinely out in the mountains. It's a spot that rewards campers who like their mornings loud with river noise and their afternoons spent on foot, not scrolling through a phone with no signal anyway.

## Who This Campground Is Built For

Tone Valley Campground works best for three kinds of campers, and it's worth being honest about which one you are before you book. First, families and groups doing a classic car camp: you drive in, unload directly at your site, and set up a basecamp for two or three nights of river play, barbecue, and lazy mornings. The car-adjacent layout means you're not hauling gear down a forest path, which matters a lot if you're bringing kids, coolers, or a full kitchen setup. Second, hikers who want a valley-floor base for day trips into the surrounding Tone hills — the campground sits close enough to marked trailheads that you can be on a ridge by mid-morning and back at your tent, beer in hand, before dinner. Third, river-focused campers who just want to fish, wade, or skip stones for a weekend without committing to a full backcountry trip.

What makes this particular site distinct from Gunma's other river campgrounds is the combination of valley depth and access ease. Many of the prefecture's scenic river camps require a genuine hike or a rough forestry road to reach; Tone Valley is graded and maintained for standard passenger cars, so you get the steep-walled valley scenery — tall cedar slopes, granite riverbed, mist that sits in the gorge until mid-morning — without the approach difficulty. It's the rare site where the drive-up convenience doesn't cancel out the feeling of remoteness.

## Site Layout and River Conditions

The campground is arranged in a loop along a wide, flat river terrace, with individual car camp sites spaced to give each group a real buffer from its neighbors — this isn't a densely packed festival-style field. Most sites are grass-over-gravel, level enough for a standard dome or tunnel tent without much site prep, and each has direct or near-direct vehicle access so you can back a car or small van right up to your pitch. A smaller number of sites sit slightly elevated on a bluff above the main terrace; these cost a little more effort to walk gear up to but reward you with a better sightline down the valley and less exposure if the river runs high after rain.

The Tone River itself splits the conditions into two zones worth knowing before you arrive. Directly in front of the main camping terrace, the current slows into broad, shallow pools — good for wading, cooling drinks, and letting younger kids splash around under supervision, though it is still a natural river and depth changes fast after upstream rain. About ten minutes' walk downstream, the river narrows and picks up speed through a rockier stretch that's popular with anglers working for ayu (sweetfish) and yamame trout in season, and with more experienced waders who want moving water rather than pool water. There is no lifeguard presence anywhere on site, so treat every stretch of the river as unsupervised open water and check current conditions at the campground office before letting anyone swim, especially after typhoon season rain.

Firepits are available at most car camp sites, and open fires directly on the ground are not permitted — bring a raised fire stand or rent one at check-in. Water access points and basic toilet facilities are spread along the loop road so no site is more than a short walk from either.

## Season, Weather, and Getting There from Tokyo

Tone Valley Campground runs a full spring-through-autumn season, roughly April through November, with the sweet spot falling in late spring (May–June, before the rainy season thickens) and autumn (late September through early November, when the valley slopes turn deep red and gold and daytime heat drops off). Midsummer (late July–August) is popular for the river access and cooler valley air compared to the Kanto plain, but it's also the busiest and most humid stretch — book well ahead and expect afternoon thunderstorms rolling off the mountains most days. Winter camping is not offered at most sites here; the valley floor gets cold overnight and river-adjacent ground frosts hard, so this is a three-season destination rather than a year-round one.

Getting there from Tokyo is straightforward by car, which fits the car-camp format well: figure roughly two to two and a half hours via the Kan-etsu Expressway, exiting toward the Numata or Minakami area and following prefectural roads into the valley. Without a car, the trip is possible but slower — take a JR shinkansen or limited express to Jomo-Kogen or Numata Station, then transfer to a local bus running toward the valley, followed by either a short taxi ride or a pre-arranged campground pickup if offered; check current bus schedules before relying on this route, as valley bus services run infrequently outside peak season. Given the site's car-camp design and the amount of gear most people bring, driving is the practical default, and a car also lets you make quick supply runs into Numata or Minakami town, both roughly 20–30 minutes away by road, for anything you forgot.

## Booking, Gear, and Trail-Access Tips

Reserve your site as early as you can for weekends between July and mid-August and for the peak autumn foliage weeks in October — Gunma's river valleys are a well-known summer escape for Tokyo residents, and popular car camp sites fill up fast once school holidays start. Weekday stays are far easier to book last-minute and also give you a quieter river to yourself.

For gear, pack as if you're camping streamside even if your site sits up on the terrace: humidity off the water means everything from tent floors to firewood benefits from a full groundsheet and a dry-bag system for spare clothes. Bring water shoes rather than relying on sandals if you plan to wade — the riverbed is mixed sand and rounded stone, and it's easy to turn an ankle barefoot. A raised firepit stand, as noted above, is close to mandatory, and bug protection (both spray and a head net for dusk) makes a real difference from June through September when the valley's river-adjacent insects are at their worst.

If hiking is part of your plan, check trailhead conditions and rough distances at the campground office before setting out — trail signage in the surrounding hills is adequate but not dense, and cell signal thins out fast once you climb away from the valley floor. Start day hikes early enough to be back well before dusk, since the terrain shifts from clear valley trail to shaded forest quickly and headlamps are easy to forget when you left them in the tent. For food, a car camp setup here works well with a simple two-burner stove and coals for the firepit rather than trying to haul in a full kitchen; Numata and Minakami both have solid grocery stops for last-minute supplies, and picking up local Gunma produce — konnyaku, mountain vegetables, and river fish from local shops — on the way in beats anything you'd pack from Tokyo. Finally, respect quiet hours strictly: sound carries unusually far across a river valley at night, and most complaints at sites like this come from late fires and speakers rather than anything structural about the campground itself.
