## Who Tonaki Pebble Bottom Is Really For

Tonaki Pebble Bottom sits off Tonaki Island, a speck of land roughly 40 kilometers west of the Kerama Islands and well off the radar of the day-trip crowds that pack Chatan or Onna. This is a site for divers who have already logged their open-water hours in Okinawa's more famous spots and want something quieter — a place where the dive boat might carry four people instead of twenty, and where the only sound once you descend is your own regulator.

What sets this site apart isn't dramatic topography. There's no cavern, no wreck, no vertical wall plunging into the blue. Instead, the draw is the seafloor itself: a wide, gently sloping bed of smooth pale pebbles, scoured clean by current and swell, that gives the whole site an almost lunar openness. Against that pale backdrop, coral colonies — table corals, branching Acropora, and clusters of soft coral swaying in the current — stand out with a clarity that's hard to find elsewhere in the prefecture. Visibility here regularly exceeds 25 meters on a good day, occasionally pushing past 30, because the pebble bottom doesn't stir up silt the way sandy or muddy sites do when fins or current pass over it.

This makes Tonaki Pebble Bottom especially good for underwater photographers and anyone who wants to practice buoyancy and macro work without fighting murk. It's also a strong pick for intermediate divers who want their first taste of a current-influenced outer-island site without jumping straight into Kerama's more demanding drift dives. Beginners are welcome on calm days with a guide, but this isn't a resort-course training pool — expect open ocean conditions, real current, and a boat ride to get there.

## Conditions, Layout, and What the Dive Plan Looks Like

The site is typically dived as a drift or semi-drift dive depending on tide and wind. Entry is usually a backward roll off a small dive boat anchored or drifting near the edge of the pebble shelf, with the bottom sitting between 8 and 18 meters depending on exactly where the boat positions you — shallow enough for long bottom times, deep enough to feel like a real dive rather than a snorkel extension.

The layout breaks into three loose zones. Close to the drop-in point, the pebble bed is nearly flat and scattered with isolated coral bommies — good for a controlled descent and a gear check before the current picks up. Moving further out, the pebbles give way to a denser coral garden where reef fish congregate: parrotfish grazing the coral heads, clouds of damselfish over the branching formations, and the occasional reef shark or eagle ray cruising the open water above the pebbles, drawn by the same clarity that makes the site attractive to divers. Along the outer edge, a mild drop-off leads toward deeper blue water, and this is where current tends to be strongest — guides generally turn the group back here rather than pushing past it.

Current is the main variable to plan around. On neap tides it can be barely noticeable, letting divers hover and shoot photos at will. On spring tides, especially during ebb, it builds into a steady drift that turns the dive into an easy glide along the coral garden — fun, but not the moment to practice hovering still for a macro shot. Local operators check tide tables closely and will often shift the dive time by an hour or two to catch the calmer window. Surface conditions matter too: because the approach from the main harbor runs across open channel water, swell above about 1.5 meters makes the crossing uncomfortable and can push operators to cancel or reschedule rather than risk a rough ride out.

Water temperature follows the seasonal pattern across the island chain — the comfortable range for most of the year, dropping into cooler territory only in the depth of winter. A 3mm wetsuit is standard for most of the year; a 5mm or a hooded vest is worth packing for winter trips.

## When to Go and How to Reach Tonaki Island

The dive window at Tonaki Pebble Bottom runs essentially year-round, but conditions and comfort vary sharply by season. Late spring through early autumn (May through October) gives the warmest water and the calmest average seas, with the narrow stretch from late May to mid-June being rainy season and worth checking forecasts closely — storms can cancel crossings with little notice. Summer brings warm water and good visibility but also typhoon risk, particularly from July through September; it's worth building a flex day into any trip scheduled in that window. Winter (December to February) offers the clearest, calmest water on the best days, since fewer typhoon systems pass through, but cooler temperatures and more frequent wind events mean a higher chance of cancellation, and a thicker wetsuit is essential.

Getting to the dive site starts with getting to Tonaki Island itself, which has no bridge connection and is reached only by sea or air from the Okinawa mainland. From Naha Airport, the most common route is a taxi or rental car to Naha Tomari Port (Tomarin), about 20–30 minutes depending on traffic. From there, a regular ferry run by the Tonaki Village ferry service covers the roughly 60 kilometers to Tonaki Port in about 2 hours, with service typically once or sometimes twice daily depending on the season — booking the ferry in advance is strongly recommended since schedules are limited and seats fill up around weekends and holidays. For those short on time, a small propeller flight connects Naha Airport to Tonaki Airport in around 20–25 minutes, though seats are limited and weather-dependent cancellations are common given the short runway and small aircraft used on the route.

Once on Tonaki Island, the dive shop and harbor are both small enough that a short walk or a quick call for a pickup covers the distance from most guesthouses. There's no need for a car once you're on the island itself, though a rental scooter is a pleasant way to see the rest of Tonaki between dives.

## Practical Tips: Booking, Gear, Stay, and Etiquette

Because Tonaki Island has only a handful of dive operators and limited ferry capacity, booking ahead is not optional — this is not a walk-up dive site. Reserve both your ferry or flight seat and your dive slot at least a week in advance in shoulder season, and two to three weeks ahead during Golden Week, summer school holidays, and any long weekend. Most local shops run small groups of two to six divers and can arrange full gear rental (BCD, regulator, wetsuit, weights) for those not traveling with their own kit, which is worth taking advantage of if you flew in and want to save luggage weight.

A few gear notes specific to this site: bring reef-safe sunscreen, since the shallow entry point near the pebble shelf sits close enough to the surface that chemical runoff matters for the coral garden below. A compact dive light is useful even in daytime, since it helps pick out color in the crevices between pebbles where small reef fish and the occasional nudibranch hide. If you shoot photos, a wide lens suits the open pebble-bottom vistas better than a macro rig, though serious macro shooters will find enough small subjects in the coral clusters to justify the switch.

Etiquette here matters more than at busier sites precisely because the site stays pristine — no fin-kicking into coral, no standing on the pebble bottom even though it looks sturdy (it's still reef substrate in places), and no touching or feeding fish. Groups are small enough that guides notice and will correct divers quickly, so it's worth briefing yourself on buoyancy before the trip rather than learning it here.

For accommodation, Tonaki Island has a small number of minshuku (family-run guesthouses) and one or two modest lodges rather than resort-style hotels; most include breakfast and can arrange dinner featuring local Okinawan dishes and fresh-caught fish. Booking lodging at the same time as your ferry or flight is wise, since rooms are limited and fill alongside the same seasonal surges that affect transport. For food beyond the guesthouse, options are limited to a handful of small eateries near the port — stock up on snacks if you're an early riser heading out for a dawn boat departure, since nothing opens that early on the island itself.

Plan for at least one overnight stay rather than attempting a day trip from the mainland; between ferry timing and dive scheduling, a same-day round trip leaves barely enough time to dive once, and gives no buffer if weather pushes your crossing. Two nights lets you dive the Pebble Bottom site twice — once on an incoming tide and once on an outgoing one — which is the best way to see how dramatically the current changes the character of the same reef.
