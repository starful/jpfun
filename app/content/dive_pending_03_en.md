## An Island Divers Fly Past Naha To Reach

Kumejima sits roughly 90 kilometers west of Okinawa's main island, far enough offshore that day-trippers from Naha rarely bother, and that distance is exactly what keeps the reefs in the condition older Okinawan divers describe from thirty years ago. The island's full-time dive population is small — a handful of family-run shops rather than the resort-hotel dive counters common around Onna or Ishigaki — and most of them have been running the same handful of boats for two decades, which means their site knowledge runs deeper than a laminated map. Divers who come to Kumejima are typically repeat visitors chasing a specific target: the scalloped hammerhead sharks that stage off the island's southern point in winter, technical divers drawn to swim-through caves cut into ancient basalt, or photographers after visibility that regularly exceeds 25 meters even in shoulder season. It is not an island that markets itself to beginners, and the shops here are candid about matching site selection to certification level rather than upselling every guest to the flashiest dive.

What separates Kumejima from its Kerama and Miyako-area rivals is geology as much as marine life. The island is one of the oldest landmasses in the Ryukyu chain, built from basalt rather than the younger coral limestone that dominates much of Okinawa, and that older rock has eroded into fissures, arches, and vertical walls that host a rougher, more dramatic underwater topography than the gentle coral slopes divers find elsewhere in the prefecture. Locals sometimes call the surrounding water "Kumejima Blue," a shade a few tones deeper and more saturated than the turquoise associated with Okinawa's postcard beaches, and the difference is visible even from a boat deck on a clear day.

## Reading the Reef: Signature Dive Sites and Underwater Terrain

The best-known site is Ōdō Point (Ōdomari), a rocky promontory off the island's southern tip where scalloped hammerhead sharks gather in loose schools from roughly late January through March, drawn by the deep channel dropping to over 40 meters just offshore. Sightings are never guaranteed — shops are upfront that this is wildlife, not a feeding show — but multiple encounters per trip are common during the peak weeks, and the site has built a small but devoted following among shark specialists who fly in specifically for this window.

Away from the hammerhead season, Kumejima's terrain rewards divers who like structure over open reef. Tobishi Iwa is a submerged rock pinnacle riddled with archways and tunnels wide enough to swim through in single file, opening onto walls draped in soft coral that catch the current in slow motion. Tatami-ishi, named for its resemblance to stacked tatami mats, is a formation of hexagonal basalt columns similar in origin to the famous Hashigui-iwa rock columns on Kyushu's coast, but here submerged and colonized by anemone gardens instead of standing dry above the waterline. Nakanoshima Rock offers a more sheltered profile popular on days when the outer sites are blown out by wind, with a gentle slope from 5 to 18 meters that still turns up turtles resting under ledges.

For a surface-level counterpart to the dive sites, Hatenohama is a roughly 7-kilometer sandbar stretching between three uninhabited islets northeast of Kumejima, accessible only by boat, with no shade structures and no fresh water — many dive shops pair a Hatenohama swim stop with an afternoon two-tank trip, since the surrounding shallows double as an easy check-out dive for divers refreshing skills before tackling Ōdō Point's current. The channel between Kumejima and the neighboring islet of Ōjima produces a tidal flow strong enough that shops schedule dives here around specific tide windows rather than a fixed daily slot, since the current can run from a lazy drift to a full drift dive within the same afternoon depending on the moon phase.

## Tides, Ferries, and Timing a Trip Right

Kumejima has no bridge or rail connection to the rest of Okinawa; getting there means either a roughly 30-minute flight from Naha Airport on a small turboprop, landing at the compact Kumejima Airport, or a 3-hour car ferry departing from Naha's Tomari Port, which runs once daily and is frequently the more economical option for divers hauling their own gear bags. Neither option runs late into the evening, so most itineraries are built around a minimum three-night stay rather than a day trip — a constraint that, in practice, filters out casual visitors and keeps the island's dive boats from ever feeling oversubscribed.

Diving is possible year-round, but conditions shift meaningfully by season. January through March brings cooler water (around 20–22°C, warranting a 5mm wetsuit or drysuit for comfort) but is the only realistic window for the hammerhead gathering at Ōdō Point. April through June offers a sweet spot of calming winter swell, warming water, and consistently high visibility before the rainy season arrives. Peak summer (July–September) brings the warmest water and the best conditions for Hatenohama sandbar trips, but also typhoon risk that can cancel boat diving entirely for several days at a stretch — it's worth building a buffer day or two into any summer booking. Shops generally require advance reservations rather than walk-up bookings, partly because boat capacity is limited to a handful of vessels island-wide and partly because certain sites, including Ōdō Point, are only run when conditions and tide align.

## Where to Sleep, Eat, and Rinse Off After the Dive

Most dive shops cluster near Nakadomari and Kanegusuku on the island's south side, within a short drive of small guesthouses and minshuku (family-run inns) that cater specifically to divers — several offer gear-drying racks and outdoor rinse stations as standard, a detail worth confirming if you're traveling with rebreather or camera equipment that needs careful freshwater rinsing after every dive day. Larger hotels exist near the airport, but divers who want to minimize commute time to the boat docks tend to favor the smaller southern-coast lodgings even if the rooms are simpler.

Food on Kumejima leans heavily on what the surrounding water and soil produce: the island is known within Okinawa for kuruma ebi (prawn) farming, and several small restaurants near the port serve it grilled or in a simple miso broth alongside standard Okinawan staples like goya champuru and soki soba. Post-dive routines here are informal — expect to eat where the boat captain eats, not at a resort buffet. Because the island has no large supermarket chain, it's worth stocking up on sunscreen, reef-safe or not, and any specific snack or electrolyte preferences before arriving, since selection narrows fast once you're off the ferry. Bring cash in smaller denominations, as several of the smaller dive shops and eateries still don't accept cards, and confirm with your shop in advance whether nitrox fills are available, since not every operator on the island stocks it.
