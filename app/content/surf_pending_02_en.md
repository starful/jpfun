## A Beach Break Living in Enoshima's Shadow

Katase Nishihama sits just west of the Katase River mouth in Fujisawa, sandwiched between the touristy bustle of Enoshima Island and the quieter residential stretch of Chigasaki-bound coastline. Locals simply call it "Nishihama" to distinguish it from Katase Higashihama on the river's east side, and the two beaches have developed distinct personalities over the decades. While Higashihama draws family swimmers and shave-ice stands in summer, Nishihama has quietly become the workhorse break for Shonan-area surfers who want consistent, forgiving waves within walking distance of a train station.

What sets Nishihama apart from the dozen other beach breaks strung along Shonan's coastline is the sightline: paddling out here puts Enoshima's pine-covered silhouette and the arched Benten Bridge directly in your peripheral vision, and on clear winter mornings Mt. Fuji rises behind the lineup to the west. It's a wave you can photograph as easily as you can ride, which has made it a recurring backdrop for Japanese surf media and amateur contests staged by the local Fujisawa surf associations. The crowd skews toward Tokyo and Yokohama commuters doing dawn patrol before work, university surf club members from nearby Shonan campuses, and a steady trickle of longboarders who prefer Nishihama's softer shoulders over the punchier peaks further east toward Chigasaki.

## Sandbar Layout and Swell Behavior

Nishihama is a sand-bottom beach break with no reef or rock hazards, which keeps the entry-level risk low compared to point breaks elsewhere in Kanto. The bathymetry is shaped by the Katase River's outflow just to the east, which deposits and redistributes sand seasonally — this means the sandbar peaks shift position after typhoons and winter storms, so the "reliable" takeoff spot from one visit can move fifty meters by the next. Regulars scout the beach at low tide to read the sandbar contours before paddling out, and it's worth doing the same rather than assuming last season's peak still holds.

The beach produces primarily beginner-to-intermediate waves in the 0.5–1.2 meter range on typical wind swells generated by passing low-pressure systems in the Pacific. Typhoon season (roughly August through October) can push chest-to-head-high faces with more power and longer rides, though the window tends to be narrow — a day or two of clean typhoon swell before onshore winds or the storm's approach makes it blown out and closed-out. Wave faces here tend to be softer and more rolling than the steeper beach breaks near Chigasaki or Shichirigahama, making Nishihama genuinely well-suited to longboarding and to surfers progressing off foam boards. The most consistent peak sits roughly in front of the lifeguard tower near the beach's center, though a secondary, less-crowded peak forms closer to the river mouth on bigger swells — locals favor this one when the main peak gets crowded on weekend mornings.

Wind pattern matters more here than at exposed points: Nishihama faces roughly south-southeast, so it holds shape best in light offshore or cross-shore land breezes typical of early morning before the sea breeze kicks in around mid-morning in summer. Afternoon onshore chop is the norm from May through September, which is why the dawn patrol culture is so entrenched locally.

## Getting There and Timing Your Session

Katase Nishihama's biggest practical advantage is transit access. Enoshima Station on the Odakyu Enoshima Line and Katase-Enoshima Station on the Odakyu line both sit within a 10–15 minute walk of the beach, and the Enoden (Enoshima Electric Railway) also stops nearby at Enoshima Station, giving surfers three rail options into the same small area. From Shinjuku, the Odakyu Romancecar reaches Katase-Enoshima in about 70 minutes, making a sunrise surf-and-return-to-Tokyo day trip realistic without a car. Drivers can reach the beach via the Shonan coastal road (Route 134), with paid parking lots operating near the Enoshima causeway and along the beachfront, though spaces fill quickly on summer weekends and after any forecasted swell.

Surfing is officially permitted at Nishihama only within demarcated surf zones set by the local fishing cooperative and beach management, separate from the swimming zones that are heavily enforced during the official swimming season (roughly mid-July to the end of August). Outside that swimming season, the whole beach effectively opens to surfing with fewer zone restrictions. First-time visitors should look for the flagged boundary markers near the lifeguard tower, since paddling out through a swim zone during peak summer hours draws quick whistle warnings from beach patrol. Board rental and lesson operators cluster along the beach road behind Nishihama, several running seasonal huts that open for the warmer months and close or scale back in deep winter — calling ahead in the off-season is wise since hours are inconsistent.

Best conditions generally line up in early autumn (September–October) when typhoon-generated swell overlaps with fading summer crowds, and again in winter (December–February) when clean north-wind offshore days produce glassy, well-shaped waves under clear Fuji-view skies, albeit in cold water requiring a 4/3mm or thicker wetsuit with boots.

## Where to Eat, Sleep, and Recover After a Session

Enoshima Island itself, a five-to-ten minute walk across the Benten Bridge from Nishihama, is the area's culinary anchor — its narrow Sando shopping street is packed with shirasu (whitebait) rice bowls, a local specialty pulled fresh from Sagami Bay, alongside grilled seafood skewers and soft-serve stands that stay open into the evening. For a quieter post-surf meal without the island's tourist density, the streets directly behind Nishihama and around Katase-Enoshima Station have a scattering of small ramen shops, curry houses, and casual cafes favored by the surf-club crowd, generally cheaper and faster than anything on Enoshima proper.

Accommodation ranges widely: budget-conscious surfers often base themselves in Fujisawa city center, a short train ride inland, where business hotels run noticeably cheaper than beachfront options. Right around Enoshima and Katase, a handful of small guesthouses and surf-themed inns cater specifically to visiting surfers, some offering board storage and outdoor rinse showers as a standard amenity — worth prioritizing over a standard hotel if you're staying multiple days to surf. For longer trips, Kamakura, roughly 15 minutes further up the Enoden line, offers a wider range of hotels and ryokan alongside its temple sightseeing, making a reasonable dual-purpose base for travelers splitting time between surfing and cultural stops.

A few practical notes locals mention often: public coin showers and rinse stations are available directly on the beach promenade but tend to have lines during summer, so budget extra time; convenience stores (there are several within a five-minute walk) are the most reliable spot for a quick pre-dawn coffee since most cafes don't open until 8 or 9 a.m.; and because the sandbar shifts, checking a recent surf report or asking at one of the beachfront rental shops the morning of your session is more useful here than trusting a generic forecast app, which tends to overestimate wave height on this stretch of coast.
