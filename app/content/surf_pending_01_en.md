## A Tokyo Surfer's Escape Hatch

Chigasaki Southern Beach sits on the Shonan coast of Kanagawa Prefecture, roughly 55 kilometers southwest of central Tokyo, and it has earned an odd double identity: half working-class fishing shoreline, half cultural landmark. The "Southern" in the beach's popular name is not a compass direction — it's a nod to Southern All Stars, the band fronted by Chigasaki-born Keisuke Kuwata, whose breezy Shonan-pop anthems turned this stretch of sand into a symbol of Japanese beach culture decades before Instagram did the same job for other coastlines. Surfers who paddle out here are sharing the water with joggers doing sunrise laps, retirees walking dogs along the seawall, and tourists posing for photos with Eboshi-iwa, the black volcanic rock that pokes out of the ocean about a kilometer offshore and anchors nearly every photograph ever taken of this beach.

What makes Chigasaki distinct from the more postcard-perfect breaks further along Shonan, like Kugenuma or Zushi, is its rawness. This is a river-mouth beach break shaped by the outflow of the Sagami River, which empties into the ocean just west of the main surfing zone. That means sandbars here shift noticeably after typhoons and heavy rain, the water carries a visible tint of river sediment for a day or two after storms, and the wave shape can flip from mushy summer slop to genuinely punchy peaks within a single week. Locals treat the beach less as a scenic backdrop and more as a functioning surf laboratory — one where reading the day's sandbar is as important as checking the forecast.

## Reading the Sandbars: Eboshi-iwa and the Sagami River Mouth

The surf zone runs in a broad crescent between the Sagami River mouth and the Chigasaki fishing harbor breakwater to the east, giving surfers roughly 1.5 kilometers of beach to scan for the best peak on any given day. Three distinct zones matter here:

- **River-mouth left**, closest to the Sagami outflow, where sand deposited by the current builds a shifting bar that can throw a genuinely fast, hollow left on a solid autumn swell — though it also carries the most debris and discoloration after rain.
- **Center beach, in line with Eboshi-iwa**, the most consistent and crowded stretch, where the rock offshore acts as a natural landmark for lineup positioning; locals will tell you to "sit level with the rock's left edge" for the main peak.
- **Harbor-side break**, tucked against the eastern breakwater, which holds a cleaner, more sheltered wave when wind swings onshore elsewhere, making it the fallback spot on blown-out afternoons.

Wave quality depends heavily on typhoon activity passing south of Honshu between August and October, which sends the beach its most powerful and rideable swell of the year — waist to head-high walls with occasional overhead sets during a well-tracked storm. Outside typhoon season, Chigasaki runs on smaller wind-swell and the tail end of Pacific groundswells, typically knee to chest high, friendlier for longboarders and improving surfers than for anyone chasing barrels. The seabed is entirely sand, so there's no reef hazard, but the river mouth does produce a noticeable rip current pulling seaward, especially on an outgoing tide — a detail every visiting surfer should register before paddling out, since rescue statistics for the wider Shonan coast consistently flag river-mouth rips as the leading cause of swimmer incidents.

## Season, Access, and Logistics

Chigasaki surfs year-round, but the calendar splits into two very different personalities. Summer (July–August) brings warm 25–28°C water, small and gentle waves, and beach crowds thick with families and beginner surf schools — great for first lessons, frustrating for anyone wanting power. Autumn (September–October) is the prized window: typhoon swells stack up, water is still warm enough for a 3/2 wetsuit, and the crowd thins to committed locals. Winter (December–February) delivers the cleanest conditions of the year thanks to reliable offshore land breezes in the early morning, but water temperatures drop to around 12–15°C, demanding a full 4/3 or thicker suit, boots, and often gloves.

Access is the beach's biggest selling point relative to anywhere else on this coast. From Tokyo Station, take the JR Tokaido Line directly to Chigasaki Station — about 60–70 minutes, no transfers. From Chigasaki Station's south exit, the beach is a 15-minute walk or a 5-minute bus/taxi ride straight down the main road toward the coast; many locals simply rent a bicycle at the station for the flat, dead-straight ride to the water. Drivers coming via the Shonan Bypass or National Route 134 will find several coin parking lots clustered near the Southern Beach Chigasaki park area, though spots fill fast on summer weekends and after any promising typhoon forecast — arriving before 7am on a swell day is standard local practice. There is no surf-specific entry fee, but municipal beach facilities (cold showers, changing rooms, lockers) operate on a small paid-use basis during the official summer beach season, roughly mid-July through August; outside those dates, facilities are more limited or closed, so many surfers change discreetly in car park lots or bring a portable shower setup.

## Post-Session Fuel and Where to Crash

Chigasaki's food scene leans casual and coastal rather than polished. Along the road connecting the station to the beach, look for small shokudo (diner-style) shops serving shirasu-don — a Shonan specialty of raw or lightly boiled baby sardines piled over rice, sourced from boats working the very same coastline surfers are riding. It's the regional dish to try here, cheap, fast, and genuinely local rather than tourist-oriented. Post-surf ramen and curry shops cluster near the station for a quick warm refuel, especially valued after a cold winter dawn session.

Lodging options are modest but practical: Chigasaki itself has a handful of business hotels near the station suited to a one- or two-night surf trip, while budget-minded surfers often base themselves in nearby Fujisawa or Enoshima, both a short train ride away, for a wider choice of guesthouses and surf-focused hostels that rent boards and offer board storage. A few independently run surf shops near the beach access roads handle board and wetsuit rentals, ding repair, and — most usefully for visitors — informal local knowledge on which sandbar is working that morning, since conditions can differ meaningfully between the river-mouth side and the harbor side on the same swell.

Practical tips worth remembering: check the tide chart alongside the swell forecast, since the river-mouth rip strengthens noticeably on a dropping tide; avoid entering the water for at least a day after heavy rain given runoff from the Sagami River; and treat the stretch directly in front of Eboshi-iwa as the most crowded lineup on the beach, with quieter alternatives available a few hundred meters east toward the harbor breakwater for surfers who'd rather skip the scrum.
