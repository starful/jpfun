## Why Kotohiki Beach Stands Out in the Setouchi Surf Scene

Most surfers picture the Seto Inland Sea as flat, sheltered water good for ferries and oyster rafts — not surfing. Kotohiki Beach quietly breaks that stereotype. Positioned along a stretch of coastline in Kagawa Prefecture where a wider mouth to the open sea lets a modest but usable swell wrap in, this beach has become the go-to local spot for surfers who want something calmer than the Pacific-facing breaks of Shikoku's south coast. The wave here is short, soft, and forgiving — exactly the kind of playground that lets nervous beginners stand up for the first time without getting hammered.

What makes Kotohiki different from a generic beach break is the combination of a fine, even sandy bottom, a gently sloping approach, and a genuine beginner surf school culture that has grown up around it. Unlike reef or rock-bottom breaks where a wipeout means a scraped knee or worse, falling here just means a mouthful of saltwater and a walk back out. Local instructors have leaned into this identity, turning what could be dismissed as a "small wave day" spot into a legitimate training ground where people learn to read whitewater, practice pop-ups, and build wave count before ever attempting the more serious breaks elsewhere in Shikoku or across the strait in Wakayama and Tokushima.

This is a spot for people who want to learn to surf without drama: families with kids trying a board for the first time, solo travelers ticking off a bucket-list lesson, intermediate surfers who live nearby and want a low-commitment evening session, and longboard riders who prefer soft, rolling faces over punchy barrels. It is not the place to go if you're chasing overhead swell or hollow tubes — for that, you'll want to drive south to the open Pacific coast. But if your goal is consistent, low-stress wave time in a scenic inland-sea setting, Kotohiki delivers exactly that, and it does so with far less crowd pressure than the well-known breaks on Japan's Pacific side.

## Reading the Break: Swell, Bottom, and How the Waves Actually Work

Kotohiki Beach is a classic beach break, meaning the wave shape is dictated entirely by sandbars that shift with tides, storms, and seasonal currents rather than a fixed reef or point. The sandy bottom is soft and generally free of rocks, which is the single biggest reason instructors recommend it for absolute beginners — falling is part of the learning process, and here it costs you nothing but a rinse.

Because Kotohiki sits within the Seto Inland Sea system, the swell window is narrower and gentler than what you'd find on an open-ocean coast. Waves are wind-driven more often than they are the product of distant groundswell, so conditions respond quickly to local weather. A few practical notes on how the break behaves:

- **Wave size**: Typically knee-to-waist high, occasionally chest-high during a strong seasonal wind event or the tail end of a passing typhoon system further offshore. This is a spot built around small, mushy waves rather than powerful ones.
- **Wave shape**: Waves tend to break close to shore and reform in sections, giving beginners multiple chances to catch whitewater on a single set rather than one clean peeling face. Longboarders and SUP surfers can often find soft, rolling shoulders further out on bigger days.
- **Tide sensitivity**: Because the sandbars are shallow and shift seasonally, the wave quality can change noticeably between mid and low tide. A rising tide generally softens the break further, which is good for lessons; a lower tide can expose slightly punchier close-out sections better suited to those with some experience.
- **Crowd factor**: Even on a good wind-swell day, Kotohiki rarely gets crowded the way famous breaks do. Most sessions you'll be sharing the lineup with a handful of local surfers and whichever school group is running a lesson that morning.
- **Water quality and scenery**: Being part of the Seto Inland Sea, the water is calmer and often clearer than open-ocean beach breaks, with small islands visible on the horizon — a distinctly Setouchi backdrop that photographs beautifully even on a mellow surf day.

For beginners, the standard approach is simple: start in the whitewater close to shore, get comfortable popping up on broken waves, and only paddle out past the breaking zone once you're confidently catching whitewater consistently. Instructors at the local beginner school build lessons around exactly this progression, usually spending the first session entirely in the shallows before ever paddling someone into unbroken swell.

## When to Go and How to Get There from Takamatsu or Okayama

**Best season**: Kotohiki Beach surfs best from late spring through autumn, roughly April through October, when seasonal wind patterns and occasional typhoon-generated swell push more consistent energy into the inland sea. Summer (July–September) brings the warmest water and the most reliable lesson conditions — ideal if you're a first-timer who wants comfortable temperatures alongside gentle waves. Early autumn, including September and October, can bring a bonus: distant typhoon systems passing south of Japan sometimes send a longer-period swell into the Seto Inland Sea, briefly boosting wave size and quality beyond the summer average. Winter is generally too flat and cold for casual surfing here, with only the most dedicated local surfers paddling out in wetsuits during rare wind events.

**Getting there**: Kotohiki Beach is located in Kagawa Prefecture, and the most practical hub for travelers is Takamatsu, the prefecture's main city and transport gateway.

- **From Takamatsu Airport**: A rental car is the most flexible option, putting you at the beach in roughly 45–60 minutes depending on traffic and the exact access road to the coast. This is the recommended route for surfers traveling with their own board bag or racks, since Japanese trains and buses are not board-friendly.
- **From Takamatsu Station (JR)**: If you're relying on public transport, take a local JR line toward the coastal area nearest Kotohiki, then transfer to a bus or taxi for the final stretch to the beach. Budget around 60–90 minutes door to door, and confirm the last train back if you're planning an evening session.
- **From Okayama**: Okayama, on Honshu, is a major Shinkansen hub and a common entry point for travelers coming from Osaka or Tokyo. From Okayama Station, it's roughly a 60–90 minute drive across the Seto Ohashi Bridge into Kagawa, making a rental car pickup in Okayama a solid alternative if you're combining this trip with other Setouchi destinations like Naoshima or the Shimanami Kaido.
- **By car in general**: Because Kotohiki is a beach-break spot without a train station directly at the shoreline, driving is genuinely the easiest way to arrive with boards, wetsuits, and gear intact, and it gives you the freedom to check conditions at a couple of nearby beaches if Kotohiki happens to be flat that day.

Plan for a full day if this is your first visit — check the tide and wind forecast the morning of, and build in flexibility to shift your session time if the wind direction isn't cooperating.

## Booking a Lesson, Gear, and Local Etiquette

**Booking your lesson**: The beginner surf school operating out of Kotohiki Beach typically runs seasonal lessons from late spring through early autumn, with the highest availability in July and August. Group lessons are the standard format and the most affordable way to get board, wetsuit, and instruction bundled together, but private lessons are usually available on request for those wanting more one-on-one coaching time or a flexible schedule. Because this is a smaller operation compared to major surf towns, it's worth reserving a day or two ahead during peak summer weekends, and confirming the meeting point directly, since exact lesson locations can shift slightly with sandbar and tide conditions.

**Gear**: Soft-top longboards are the standard rental for beginners here, chosen specifically because their buoyancy and forgiving rails suit the gentle, mushy nature of the wave. If you're bringing your own board, a longboard or a mid-length funboard will get far more use out of a typical Kotohiki session than a short, high-performance shortboard. Wetsuit needs vary by season: a spring suit or shorty is usually enough in the height of summer, while a full 3/2mm suit is worth packing for shoulder-season sessions in late spring or October when the water starts to cool.

**Food and stay**: Kagawa Prefecture is famous nationwide for sanuki udon, and there's no better post-surf reward than a warm bowl at one of the countless small udon shops scattered around Takamatsu and the surrounding towns — many locals treat this as an essential part of any Kagawa day trip. For accommodation, Takamatsu offers the widest range of hotels and guesthouses and makes a comfortable base if you're also planning to visit the Shikoku Mura open-air museum, Ritsurin Garden, or take a ferry out to the art islands of the Seto Inland Sea. If you want to stay closer to the water for an early session, look for smaller guesthouses or minshuku near the coastal towns closer to Kotohiki itself, which cut down significantly on morning travel time.

**Etiquette**: Because the lineup is shared between a beginner school and local surfers, a few courtesies go a long way. Give lesson groups space near the shorebreak — they're often working in a tight zone right at the whitewater line. Don't drop in on someone already riding a wave, even a small one; the etiquette rules of surfing apply just as much on a gentle beach break as they do on a world-class point. Rinse and stow your gear away from walking paths on the beach, and be mindful that this is a working coastal community as well as a surf spot — respect any posted local guidance about parking or beach access, since space right at the shoreline can be limited during peak summer weekends.
