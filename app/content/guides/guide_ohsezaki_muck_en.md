## Introduction: Japan's Muck Diving Capital

Tucked along the western coast of the Izu Peninsula, a short drive from Numazu and roughly two hours from Tokyo, Ohsezaki (大瀬崎) has quietly built a reputation as one of the finest muck diving shore dive sites in all of Japan — and arguably in the wider Asia-Pacific muck diving circuit that includes Lembeh and Anilao. For divers who have grown tired of chasing reef fish across coral gardens and instead crave the thrill of spotting a paper-thin ghost pipefish clinging to a strand of algae, or a juvenile frogfish the size of a fingernail hiding in volcanic sand, Ohsezaki delivers in spades.

What makes Ohsezaki special is its geology. The site sits on a narrow, hooked peninsula formed by ancient volcanic activity from nearby Mount Fuji's eruptive history, creating a seabed of black volcanic sand, scattered rubble, and drop-off walls that plunge into deep water just meters from shore. This combination — sheltered bays on both sides of the peninsula, black sand substrate, and nutrient-rich currents funneling through Suruga Bay — creates ideal conditions for the kind of cryptic, camouflage-dependent marine life that macro photographers and muck diving enthusiasts travel across the world to find. Add to this Japan's famously meticulous dive shop culture, excellent shore access, and a temperate-to-subtropical mix of species (thanks to the warm Kuroshio Current brushing past Izu), and you have a site that rewards patient, observant divers again and again, dive after dive, season after season.

This guide covers everything you need to plan a successful muck diving trip to Ohsezaki: the best entry points, what critters to expect and when, how to prepare for night dives, and how the site fits into the broader outdoor and adventure travel culture of the Izu Peninsula — a region equally beloved by surfers, campers, and hot spring travelers.

## Ohsezaki in the History of Japanese Diving Culture

Ohsezaki's connection to the sea predates recreational scuba diving by centuries. The peninsula is home to Ose Shrine (大瀬神社), a Shinto shrine dedicated to the sea god, and the site has long been considered spiritually significant to local fishing communities who relied on Suruga Bay's exceptionally deep, cold waters for their livelihood. Suruga Bay itself is the deepest bay in Japan, plunging to over 2,500 meters not far offshore, and this proximity to the abyss is part of why unusual and deep-water species occasionally drift into the shallows at Ohsezaki, delighting divers with rare sightings not typically found at shallower, more tropical dive destinations.

Ohsezaki also holds an unusual botanical curiosity: a freshwater pond called Ike, located just meters from the ocean's edge on the peninsula, filled with carp and surrounded by a rare grove of Bikanikoyamaki (a species of tree found almost nowhere else in central Japan). Local legend holds that any fish placed in Ike will mysteriously disappear overnight — attributed to underground connections to the sea. This juxtaposition of sacred freshwater pond and volcanic seabed has made Ohsezaki a place of pilgrimage for both spiritual travelers and divers for generations.

Scuba diving took off here in the late 20th century as Japanese divers and dive shop operators began to recognize the extraordinary macro biodiversity along the shoreline, particularly the seasonal appearances of frogfish, seahorses, nudibranchs, and juvenile fish using the volcanic sand as nursery grounds. Today, Ohsezaki is considered a rite of passage for serious Japanese macro photographers and has increasingly drawn international muck diving specialists who once considered the Philippines and Indonesia the only serious macro destinations in the region. The site's enduring popularity — despite Izu's cooler water temperatures compared to tropical muck diving meccas — speaks to the sheer density and rarity of subjects found here.

## Where to Dive and How: Practical Shore Entry and Logistics Tips

**Getting there:** Ohsezaki sits on the Izu Peninsula in Shizuoka Prefecture. From Tokyo, drive via the Tomei Expressway to Numazu, then follow local roads roughly 30–40 minutes to the peninsula's base, or take the JR Tokaido Line to Numazu Station and connect via local bus or taxi. Most divers rent a car, since equipment transport and access to multiple dive points is far easier with your own vehicle.

**The two main shore dive zones:**
- **Ichiban ("First Point") and Sanban ("Third Point")** on the outer (ocean-facing) side of the peninsula offer deeper walls, stronger currents, and better visibility — ideal for divers seeking pelagic encounters alongside macro subjects.
- **The bay side (inner cove)**, sheltered from swell, is the classic muck diving zone: flat black sand slopes, minimal current, and superb conditions for slow, methodical macro hunting and night diving. This is where most dedicated muck divers spend the bulk of their bottom time.

**Shore entry technique:** Entries are typically over sand or small volcanic pebble beaches, making Ohsezaki very approachable even for less experienced divers, though some points have a short walk over rocks that calls for sturdy boots or dive booties. Local dive shops (many clustered near the peninsula's entrance) provide tanks, weights, freshwater rinse tanks, hot showers, and — critically in cooler months — hot water for warming up between dives. Booking through one of these shops, rather than attempting a fully independent dive, is strongly recommended for first-time visitors, as staff can brief you on current conditions, recent macro sightings, and safe entry points.

**Best seasons:** Water temperatures range from around 14–16°C (57–61°F) in winter to 24–27°C (75–81°F) in late summer. A 7mm wetsuit or drysuit is standard for winter and spring diving; a 3–5mm suit suffices in summer. Many of the rarest macro finds — including juvenile frogfish, ornate ghost pipefish, and various seasonal nudibranch blooms — appear in late autumn through spring, when the water is colder but visibility and critter density are often at their peak. Summer brings warmer water and more juvenile tropical strays carried in by the Kuroshio Current, alongside the peak season for surfing and camping elsewhere on the peninsula.

**Night diving:** Ohsezaki's night dives are legendary among Japanese muck diving circles. The bay-side sand flats come alive after dark with hunting octopus, bobbit worms, sleeping parrotfish, and a dramatically different cast of nocturnal nudibranchs and crustaceans compared to daytime dives. Bring a strong primary dive light plus a backup, and consider a focus light for photography strobes. Because entries are shore-based and shallow, night diving here is far less logistically demanding than boat-based night dives elsewhere, making it an excellent introduction to night muck diving for divers who have not tried it before.

**Combining with other Izu adventures:** The Izu Peninsula is a year-round outdoor playground beyond diving. Surfers flock to breaks near Shimoda and the peninsula's eastern coast in typhoon swell season (August–October), while the interior and coastal campgrounds near Izu Kogen and Amagi offer excellent camping under cedar forests, often paired with visits to the region's abundant onsen (hot spring) towns. In winter, some visitors combine an Izu dive trip with a day trip north to the ski resorts around Gotemba or the greater Fuji Five Lakes area, using Numazu as a convenient base connecting sea and mountain.

## Expert Recommendations for Serious Macro and Muck Divers

Veteran Ohsezaki divers and local guides consistently offer the same core advice: slow down. Muck diving rewards divers who move at a fraction of the pace of typical reef diving, scanning the same square meter of sand for minutes at a time rather than cruising past it. Here are specific recommendations from experienced macro photographers who dive Ohsezaki regularly:

- **Hire a local guide for your first few dives.** Ohsezaki's critters are experts in camouflage, and a guide who dives the site weekly will spot species you would swim right past — a nearly invisible juvenile frogfish, a robust ghost pipefish mimicking dead leaves, or a tiny hairy shrimp on a sea urchin's spine.
- **Bring a macro lens or macro wet lens setup**, ideally with a focus light and a snoot for creative lighting on night dives. Wide-angle photography has limited application here compared to true macro and super-macro gear.
- **Perfect your buoyancy before you arrive.** Volcanic sand kicks up easily and ruins visibility for the rest of your group; hovering motionless is essential muck diving etiquette and a core skill for getting quality photographs.
- **Dive the same spot across multiple days.** Because much of the marine life here is resident or seasonal rather than transient, returning to the same patch of sand on consecutive dives often reveals subjects you missed, or documents behavioral changes like a frogfish that has moved twenty centimeters overnight.
- **Check with dive shops for current sighting boards.** Japanese shops at Ohsezaki are known for meticulously tracking recent finds (a practice similar to sighting logs at Lembeh Strait resorts), and checking these boards before a dive dramatically improves your odds of finding sought-after species like seahorses or rare nudibranchs.
- **Don't neglect the night dive**, even if you are primarily a daytime shooter — many of Ohsezaki's signature muck subjects, including hunting cephalopods and certain crustaceans, are essentially nocturnal and rarely seen on day dives.
- **Layer up for surface intervals.** Izu's coastal winds can make surface intervals colder than the water itself in winter months; a warm change of clothes and thermos of tea makes a noticeable difference in comfort between dives.

## Conclusion: A Macro Diver's Pilgrimage Site

Ohsezaki has earned its place among the world's great muck diving destinations not through dramatic reef structure or crystal-clear tropical water, but through the sheer density and diversity of small, strange, and beautifully camouflaged life hiding in its black volcanic sand. It is a site that teaches patience and rewards close observation, a shore dive that is as accessible to a confident open-water diver as it is endlessly engaging to a veteran macro photographer chasing that one perfect shot of a paper-thin ghost pipefish under a red focus light. Combined with the surrounding Izu Peninsula's rich menu of surf breaks, forested campgrounds, hot springs, and easy access to Tokyo, a diving trip to Ohsezaki can easily anchor a broader Japanese outdoor adventure — one where the shore, the mountain, and the sea all meet within a single day's travel. For anyone serious about muck diving, night diving, or simply expanding their macro portfolio, Ohsezaki deserves a place at the very top of the Izu itinerary.
