## Who the Fuji Five Lakes Circuit Is For

A circuit camp through the Fuji Five Lakes (Fujigoko) is built for travelers who want more than one postcard shot of Mt. Fuji — it's for people willing to pack up a tent every one or two nights and chase the mountain's reflection across five different lakes. This isn't a single-base weekend at Kawaguchiko with day trips; it's a loop itinerary that treats the whole region as a trail of campsites, each with its own angle on Fuji, its own lake-edge activity, and its own microclimate.

It suits campers with a car (or willingness to rely on the Fuji Five Lakes loop bus) who are comfortable striking camp repeatedly, plus anyone who wants variety: Kawaguchiko's lively onsen-town energy, Saiko's quiet forest sites near the Aokigahara edge, Shojiko's small, uncrowded shoreline, Motosuko's famous "thousand-yen-bill view" of Fuji, and Yamanakako's windsurfing and SUP culture. Families who want one easy multi-night base should skip the full circuit and just pick Kawaguchiko or Yamanakako; this itinerary is for people who want the complete lap.

Historically, the Fuji Five Lakes formed when lava flows from Mt. Fuji's eruptions dammed ancient rivers, splitting one larger lake into the five basins seen today. The region has long been a pilgrimage corridor — Edo-period Fuji-ko pilgrims climbed the mountain and circled the lakes performing ritual purification, and shrines like Fuji Sengen Jinja near Kawaguchiko still mark those old routes. Mt. Fuji and its surrounding lakes are inscribed together as a UNESCO World Heritage cultural site specifically for this pilgrimage and artistic legacy (think Hokusai's woodblock prints), which gives a circuit camp a layer of cultural texture beyond scenery — you're tracing a route people have walked for centuries.

## Conditions: Route Order, Camp Layout, and Lakeside Activities

A clean circuit runs clockwise or counterclockwise from Kawaguchiko, hitting all five lakes in three to five nights:

- **Lake Kawaguchiko** — the hub. Campsites and glamping-style sites (PICA Fujiyama, Kawaguchiko Shinsei camp) sit on the north shore with unobstructed Fuji views across the water, especially near Oishi Park. This is also where you'll find gear shops, the Kachi Kachi Yama ropeway for an elevated Fuji panorama, and the best resupply options before heading further out.
- **Lake Saiko** — the forested, quiet option. Saiko Iyashi no Sato and the Saiko Koyo-dai area have simple lakeside sites tucked against dense woodland bordering Aokigahara forest. Fewer services, more solitude; good for a slower night two of the circuit.
- **Lake Shojiko** — the smallest and least developed lake, with Panorama-dai above it giving one of the best classic Fuji-and-lake photo compositions in the region. Camp options here are limited and basic, which is part of the appeal if you're circuit camping for variety rather than comfort.
- **Lake Motosuko** — the postcard lake, literally: its Fuji view from the western shore is the image printed on the old Japanese 1,000-yen note. Lakeside camp layout is mostly flat grass pitches close to the water; this is a strong fishing and canoeing stop and arguably the circuit's visual highlight.
- **Lake Yamanakako** — the largest lake and the activity lake. Windsurfing, SUP rental, and swan-boat tourism cluster here, plus Hananomiyako Park's seasonal flower fields with Fuji as backdrop. Campsites (Yamanakako Hananomiyako camp, Ichiban-Mura) tend to be bigger and more family-oriented, making it a good closing or opening night with easier logistics before or after the quieter lakes.

For camp layout across all five, expect car-accessible sites with gravel or grass pitches, shared water points, and coin-operated showers rather than full bathhouses — pack a quick-dry towel and plan on using nearby onsen (several dot the Kawaguchiko and Yamanakako shorelines) for a proper wash. None of the lakes offer technical diving, surfing swell, or ski terrain on-site, but Fuji's broader flank connects to winter activity at Fujiten Snow Resort (accessible from Yamanakako/Kawaguchiko) if you're bridging a camp trip into a ski day, and Lake Yamanakako's open water is the closest thing to a "surf" culture in the area via windsurfing when autumn winds pick up.

## Season and Access: When to Go and How to Reach the Lakes

**Best season:** Late April–May and late September–November are the sweet spots. Spring brings clear mornings and the Fuji Shibazakura (moss phlox) festival near Motosuko; autumn brings crisp air, the best statistical odds of a cloud-free Fuji at sunrise, and koyo (fall foliage) around Saiko and Motosuko. Summer (July–August) is warm enough for swimming and SUP at Yamanakako but Fuji is frequently clouded over by midday, and campsites book out fast around Japanese holiday weeks (Golden Week in early May, Obon in mid-August). Winter camping is possible at a few year-round sites near Kawaguchiko and Yamanakako but expect sub-zero nights and a four-season tent plus a proper sleeping bag rating — most casual circuit campers should avoid December–February unless specifically chasing diamond-dust clear skies and snow-capped Fuji shots.

**Access from Tokyo (the real hub):** The whole circuit starts and ends most efficiently at Kawaguchiko.
- **By train:** JR Chuo Line limited express from Shinjuku to Otsuki (~1h), then transfer to the Fujikyuko Line to Kawaguchiko Station (~1h). Total roughly 2 hours, 2–2.5 with waits.
- **By highway bus:** Direct Fujikyu/Keio highway buses run from Shinjuku Station (Busta Shinjuku) straight to Kawaguchiko in about 1h45m–2h, often cheaper than the train and the simplest option if you're hauling camping gear.
- **By car:** Chuo Expressway from central Tokyo to the Kawaguchiko IC takes about 1h30m–2h depending on traffic; a car is genuinely the best choice for a multi-site circuit since public transit between the five lakes (the Fuji Five Lakes loop bus network) adds significant time and doesn't always run close to campsite entrances.
- **Between lakes:** If you're car-free, the Fujikyu "Retro Bus" lines loop around Kawaguchiko and out to Saiko, Shojiko, and Motosuko on a few scheduled routes per day; Yamanakako has its own separate loop bus from Kawaguchiko/Mishima side. Budget extra transfer time and check the last-bus schedule before you pitch camp for the night, since rural runs can stop as early as 5–6pm.

## Practical Tips: Booking, Gear, and Etiquette for a Multi-Site Circuit

**Booking:** Reserve each campsite individually and in advance, especially Kawaguchiko and Yamanakako sites, which fill first on weekends and during cherry blossom or foliage season. Japanese campsite booking portals (and many sites' own phone lines) often require a same-day or day-before cutoff, so build buffer days into your itinerary rather than planning a tight back-to-back five-night chain — weather or a sold-out site can force you to double up a night somewhere.

**Gear for the loop:** Pack light enough to break camp efficiently each morning — a smaller 2–3 person tent, a compressible sleeping system, and a camp stove that doesn't need frequent canister swaps (fuel canisters are sold at convenience stores in Kawaguchiko and Fujiyoshida but are scarcer near Saiko and Shojiko). A car roof box or large duffel beats a backpack for this style of trip since you're not hiking between sites. Bring layers regardless of season — lake-basin nights run noticeably colder than Tokyo, even in summer, because of the elevation (all five lakes sit between roughly 800–1,000m).

**Food and resupply:** Kawaguchiko and Yamanakako have supermarkets and convenience stores; Saiko, Shojiko, and Motosuko have far fewer, so buy two to three days of food before you leave the Kawaguchiko hub if your route visits the quieter lakes consecutively. Several campsites rent or sell firewood and charcoal on-site — check in advance rather than hauling your own fuel across the circuit.

**Etiquette:** Japanese campsites are strict about designated fire pits, quiet hours (typically from 9–10pm), and trash separation — bring your own trash bags since many rural sites have no on-site disposal and expect you to pack it out. Keep noise low near shared water areas, and if a site has communal bathhouse-style showers, follow the same wash-before-soaking etiquette as an onsen. Respect any shrine or pilgrimage markers you pass near Kawaguchiko and the lower lakes — the Fuji-ko pilgrimage trail still gets foot traffic from both tourists and practicing pilgrims, and the sites are treated as active cultural and religious spaces, not just scenery.
