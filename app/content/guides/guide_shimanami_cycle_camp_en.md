## Riding Between Two Seas: An Introduction to the Shimanami Kaido

The Shimanami Kaido is a 70-kilometer chain of bridges and island roads linking Onomichi on Japan's main island of Honshu to Imabari on Shikoku, threading across the Seto Inland Sea through six small islands: Mukaishima, Innoshima, Ikuchijima, Omishima, Hakatajima, and Oshima. For cyclists, it is something close to a pilgrimage route — a dedicated blue line painted along the entire course guides riders across suspension bridges suspended high above shipping lanes, past citrus groves clinging to terraced hillsides, and through fishing villages where the pace of life has barely changed in decades. Combining this ride with camping transforms it from a day trip into a slow, immersive journey, one where the sound of waves and the smell of salt air replace hotel air conditioning, and where a tent pitched on a small island beach becomes the best seat in the house for a Seto Inland Sea sunset.

Cycle-camping the Shimanami Kaido rewards those willing to carry a bit more gear in exchange for a much richer experience. Rather than racing the 70 km in a single push, spreading the ride over two or three days allows time to swim at island beaches, sample fresh seafood and local citrus, and detour onto quieter backroads away from the main cycling course. Ferries play a quietly essential role in this plan: several routes bypass the busiest bridge sections, connect otherwise isolated coves, and offer a scenic, wind-free alternative when legs are tired or weather turns. Understanding the ferry sites, timetables, and how they interact with the cycling route is one of the most valuable pieces of planning knowledge for anyone attempting this trip with a bicycle and a tent.

## From Shipping Lanes to Cycling Icon: Why the Shimanami Kaido Matters

The Seto Inland Sea has been one of Japan's most important maritime corridors for over a thousand years, a sheltered waterway connecting the ports of western Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu. Long before the bridges existed, the islands and straits of this region were controlled by the Murakami Kaizoku, a confederation of maritime clans often translated as "pirates" but functioning more like toll-collecting naval powers who protected and taxed shipping traffic through the narrow channels. Oshima island, near the Shikoku end of the route, still holds the Murakami Kaizoku Museum, and the fortified strategic points once used to monitor the strait are visible from several sections of the cycling course. This maritime heritage explains why ferries remain so deeply woven into daily life here — long before any bridge was built, boats were the only way to move people, mail, and goods between these islands.

The modern Shimanami Kaido opened in stages between 1999 and 2006, the first crossing in Japan to combine expressway, pedestrian, and cyclist infrastructure across the entire span, including a dedicated cycling and walking path fully separated from car traffic on most bridges. It quickly became a symbol of Japan's growing cycling tourism industry, drawing international attention after being named one of CNN's must-ride cycling routes. Local governments in Hiroshima and Ehime prefectures have since invested heavily in cyclist infrastructure — blue route markings, rental terminals, repair stations, and coastal campgrounds — cementing the route's identity as Japan's premier long-distance cycling course. Camping along the way connects riders to an older rhythm of island life: fishing boats leaving before dawn, small shrines tucked into hillsides, and the ferries that still, even today, form the practical backbone of getting around the inland sea.

## Planning the Route: Camp Sites, Ferry Crossings, and Logistics

**Choosing your direction and pace.** Most cyclists ride from Onomichi to Imabari, since Onomichi's ferry terminal is easy to reach by train and the route gradually builds toward Shikoku's more rugged scenery. A relaxed cycle-camping itinerary spreads the ride over two nights, covering roughly 25-35 km per day, leaving ample time for detours, beach stops, and setting up camp before dusk.

**Recommended campsites along the way.**
- **Sunset Beach (Mukaishima)** — a short ride from Onomichi's ferry terminal, this free beachside campground is a popular first-night stop, with showers and toilets on site and genuinely spectacular sunsets over the strait.
- **Kohara Beach Camping Ground (Innoshima)** — a low-cost, well-maintained site right on the sand, ideal for a swim after a day of riding; reservations are recommended in July and August.
- **Ikuchijima Sunset Beach** — near the island's citrus orchards and the famous Kosanji Temple complex, this site offers a quieter atmosphere and easy access to the island's hot spring facility for a post-ride soak.
- **Amanogawa Park Camp Site (Omishima)** — set back slightly from the coast, this shaded site is a good option in peak summer heat, with the Oyamazumi Shrine within cycling distance.

Always check ahead: many of these sites operate seasonally, close in winter months, and may require advance reservation by phone during obon and summer holidays.

**Where ferries fit in.** Ferry sites are scattered at nearly every major crossing point along the route and are essential for cycle-campers for three reasons: they shorten the ride on tired-leg days, they connect to islands off the main course that are worth a detour, and they serve as a backup when strong winds make the exposed bridge sections unsafe for cycling (several bridges close to cyclists during typhoon warnings or high wind advisories). Key ferry sites include the Onomichi–Mukaishima ferry (a short, frequent crossing used by nearly every rider starting the route, since bicycles are not permitted on the Shin-Onomichi Bridge), the Setoda–Omishima connections, and smaller local ferries around Oshima and Imabari that link fishing hamlets not served by the cycling bridges. Fares are low, typically a few hundred yen per person plus a small bicycle surcharge, and boarding with a loaded touring bike is straightforward — simply wheel it aboard and hold it steady, as most inland sea ferries have no dedicated bike racks.

**Packing for camp and coast.** Bring a compact, low-profile tent that can handle sea breezes, a sleeping pad rated for the coastal humidity of the Seto Inland Sea's shoulder seasons, and dry bags for electronics given the frequent proximity to water. A basic repair kit, lights for tunnel sections, and cash for smaller ferry terminals and rural campsites (not all accept cards) round out the essentials.

## Riding Like a Local: Expert Tips for a Smoother Crossing

Seasoned Shimanami riders recommend starting early, both to beat the midday heat on exposed bridge sections and to secure a good camping spot before it fills, especially on weekends. Renting a bicycle through the "Cross Bike" one-way rental network is worth considering for camp-only travelers who don't want to bring a full touring rig — bikes can be picked up in Onomichi and dropped off in Imabari (or vice versa) at any of the numerous rental terminals along the route, freeing up saddlebag space for camping gear instead of bike maintenance tools.

For ferry timing, download or photograph the timetables in advance, since some of the smaller island ferries run on reduced schedules outside peak tourist season and Wi-Fi can be unreliable mid-crossing. Locals also suggest riding the course counter to the prevailing wind pattern when possible — winds over the inland sea typically build through the afternoon, so an early departure not only avoids heat but also calmer water crossings if you plan to combine cycling with a swim or a short paddle from your camp beach. Finally, pack a headlamp and reflective gear: several of the island back roads connecting ferry ports to campsites are unlit, and the shift from cycling path to quiet fishing village road happens quickly after sunset.

## Setting Up Camp Under the Seto Inland Sea Sky: Final Thoughts

Cycle-camping the Shimanami Kaido offers a rare combination in Japan: engineered, world-class cycling infrastructure paired with the slow, tide-governed rhythm of small island life. The bridges deliver the adrenaline and the views, but it is the campsites and ferry crossings that give the trip its texture — a swim off a quiet beach at dusk, a short boat ride past fishing boats at anchor, a tent zipped shut to the sound of waves rather than traffic. Plan your route around the rhythm of the ferries as much as the mileage between bridges, book your camp sites ahead during peak season, and leave room in the schedule for the detours that make this route memorable. Whether ridden in a single ambitious weekend or stretched into a leisurely week of island-hopping, the Shimanami Kaido rewards cycle-campers with one of the most complete outdoor experiences the Seto Inland Sea has to offer.
