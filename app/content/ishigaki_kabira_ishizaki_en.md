## Why Serious Manta Divers Keep Coming Back to Kabira Ishizaki

Ishigaki Island has several manta points scattered along its northern coastline, but Kabira Ishizaki holds a different reputation among local dive operators than the more casual sites near Kabira Bay itself. This is not a shallow, drift-along lookout — it's a rocky point exposed to open ocean swell and current, with a cleaning station that sits deep enough to demand real buoyancy control and dive planning. The manta rays that visit here are wild, unbaited, and unpredictable, which is exactly why divers who have already logged easier manta encounters elsewhere in Okinawa come back specifically for Ishizaki. It rewards patience and skill rather than luck.

What sets this site apart from Manta Scramble or the inner bay points is the combination of topography and exposure. Ishizaki juts out into open water, creating a natural channel where nutrient-rich currents sweep past a series of rocky pinnacles and overhangs. Manta rays use this specific current line to glide in toward cleaning stations worked by butterflyfish and small wrasse. Because the point is unsheltered, conditions can shift from glassy to choppy within an hour, and boat captains make the call on the day depending on wind direction and tidal flow. This variability is part of the appeal: when conditions align, divers get long, close encounters with multiple mantas circling the same cleaning rock for twenty minutes or more, something far less common at more sheltered, heavily dived sites.

This is squarely an advanced-diver destination. Operators running trips to Ishizaki generally want to see an Advanced Open Water certification or equivalent logged dives, plus recent experience with current diving. The site isn't technically extreme — most dives sit in the 15 to 25 meter range — but the combination of current, depth, and the discipline required to hover motionless near a cleaning station without disturbing the mantas means this isn't a first-dive-of-the-trip kind of site. Guides will often brief divers on negative entries and reef hooks before the boat even leaves the harbor.

## Dive Conditions, Topography, and What to Expect Underwater

Kabira Ishizaki is built around a cluster of granite boulders and low ridges that drop from the surface down to a sandy-rubble bottom around 20 to 28 meters. The cleaning station itself — the spot where mantas hover while cleaner fish pick at their skin and gill plates — sits on a flattened rock shelf at roughly 18 to 22 meters, close enough to the current line that divers typically approach from downcurrent and settle in using a reef hook or by finding a low-profile rock to tuck behind.

Visibility is generally strong, often in the 15 to 25 meter range on good days, though it can drop noticeably when swell stirs up sediment from the shallower sections closer to Kabira Bay. Water temperature follows the seasonal pattern typical of Okinawa, ranging from around 22°C in winter to close to 29°C in high summer, so a 3mm wetsuit is comfortable for most of the year and a 5mm or semi-dry suit is worth considering for winter trips when currents keep core temperature dropping faster than expected.

Current is the defining feature of this site. It can run anywhere from barely perceptible to a strong, sustained push that requires active fin kicks or a reef hook to hold position. Dive guides read the tide tables carefully before committing to Ishizaki over other manta points, since the best manta activity tends to correlate with specific tidal exchange windows rather than being spread evenly through the day. Divers should expect drift-style entries and exits, with the boat tracking bubbles on the surface rather than divers returning to a fixed anchor line.

Beyond the mantas, the site has a healthy population of resident reef fish, occasional reef sharks patrolling the deeper drop-offs, and soft coral growth on the shaded sides of the boulders. Macro life is present but secondary — almost everyone booking this site is here for one animal, and guides structure the entire dive plan around maximizing time at the cleaning station rather than exploring the broader reef.

## Manta Season and Getting to Ishigaki

Manta sightings at Kabira Ishizaki are possible year-round, but the reliable season runs from roughly May through November, with peak activity typically reported between June and September when water temperatures are warmest and plankton density around the point is highest. Winter trips (December through March) still produce sightings, particularly during favorable tidal windows, but operators are upfront that encounters become less frequent and dive schedules depend more heavily on day-to-day conditions. If a guaranteed manta encounter is the priority, aim for a multi-day stay during peak season so a poor-visibility or no-show day doesn't sink the whole trip.

Getting to Ishigaki itself starts with a flight into New Ishigaki Airport (ISG), which has direct connections from Naha (Okinawa's main hub, about 1 hour flight time) as well as seasonal and scheduled direct flights from Tokyo (Haneda and Narita), Osaka, and Fukuoka. There is no train network on Ishigaki, so once you land, a rental car or taxi is the standard way to get around — the airport sits roughly 20 to 30 minutes by car from Ishigaki City, where most dive shops and accommodation are based.

From Ishigaki City, Kabira Bay — the jumping-off point for boats heading to Ishizaki — is about a 30 to 40 minute drive along the northern coastal road. Most dive operators either run a shuttle pickup from hotels in Ishigaki City or ask divers to meet directly at a harbor near Kabira. Because the dive site itself is only accessible by boat and the harbor is a separate stop from the famous Kabira Bay glass-boat tourist area, confirm pickup logistics with your shop in advance — some first-time visitors mistakenly head to the main Kabira Bay parking area expecting to find the dive boats there.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette for a Manta Trip Worth the Trip

Book well ahead if you're traveling during peak season (June to September), since the handful of shops running trips specifically to Kabira Ishizaki have limited boat capacity and these slots fill quickly, especially around Japanese holiday periods like Obon in mid-August. When booking, be upfront about your certification level and current-diving experience — shops take the advanced classification seriously here and may redirect less experienced divers to gentler manta sites nearer the bay rather than risk a bad experience at Ishizaki's exposed point.

For gear, bring your own well-fitted mask and a reef hook if you own one, since not every shop stocks spares and hovering near a cleaning station without disturbing silt or startling the mantas is much easier with a hook than by fighting the current with fins alone. A 3mm wetsuit covers most of the dive season comfortably; pack a hood and gloves for winter trips. A compact dive light is useful for peering into shaded crevices around the boulders even though this isn't a dedicated night or cave site.

Etiquette around the cleaning station matters more here than at most dive sites. Mantas will abort a cleaning pass if divers crowd the rock, exhale bubbles directly at them, or chase for a closer photo. The accepted practice is to settle low, stay still, and let the animals approach on their own terms — guides will signal when it's acceptable to adjust position. Avoid flash photography and keep a respectful distance of several meters; the mantas that return to this station do so because it remains undisturbed.

After diving, Ishigaki City has a strong selection of casual seafood restaurants built around the local fishing catch, plus Ishigaki beef, which rivals Wagyu from the mainland and is worth seeking out for a post-dive meal. Many dive shops are based out of small guesthouses or partner with nearby accommodation, so asking your shop for a lodging recommendation near the harbor can simplify early-morning boat departures, which are common given that the best current windows at Ishizaki often fall in the early morning hours.
