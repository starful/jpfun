## Why Divers Skip Straight Past Amami Oshima For This

Most visitors to the Amami archipelago never make it past the main island. That's exactly why Kakeroma Island has stayed one of the least-crowded dive destinations in the Kagoshima prefecture chain. Separated from Amami Oshima by the narrow Oshima Strait, Kakeroma sees a fraction of the boat traffic and diver volume of its bigger neighbor, which means the reef structures here have had decades longer to recover from anchor damage, typhoon churn, and human pressure.

This is a destination for divers who already have their basic certification dialed in and want something more textured than a resort reef. The appeal isn't a single iconic wall or wreck — it's the sheer density of healthy topography packed into a small stretch of coastline: sheltered coves for easy drift dives, narrow channels that funnel nutrient-rich current past coral bommies, and drop-offs where pelagics cruise just beyond visibility range. Local dive shops on Kakeroma run small groups, often four divers or fewer per boat, so briefings are personal and dive plans get adjusted to match conditions on the day rather than a fixed script.

It's also a place where the above-water experience matters as much as the below-water one. Kakeroma has no traffic lights, one main coastal road, and a handful of settlements strung along inlets that look more like fjords than typical Japanese coastline. Divers who come here tend to stay two or three nights minimum, not because logistics force it, but because the pace rewards it.

## Dive Site Conditions: Channels, Coral Gardens, and Current Windows

The dive sites around Kakeroma fall into three broad categories, and knowing which is running well on a given day is the job of the local guides — but it helps to understand the terrain before you book.

**The Oshima Strait channel sites** are the signature draw. Tidal exchange between the Pacific side and the strait creates current lines that concentrate plankton, which in turn draws schooling fusiliers, barracuda, and occasional hammerhead sightings in the cooler months. These are drift dives by nature: entry and exit points are current-dependent, and guides read the tide tables closely before committing to a plan. Visibility in the channel typically runs 15–25 meters, dropping when spring tides stir up sediment.

**The sheltered cove sites** on Kakeroma's southern and western coast are where most first dives of a multi-day trip happen. Shallow coral tables sit in 5–12 meters of calm water, ideal for buoyancy checks and camera setup before tackling anything current-driven. Hard coral cover here is genuinely dense — table corals, staghorn thickets, and healthy anemone clusters with resident clownfish are the norm rather than the exception.

**The outer drop-off sites** face open ocean and are conditions-dependent, usually only run when swell is under a meter and wind is light. These go deeper, often 20–30 meters along a sloping wall, and are where divers occasionally spot manta rays passing through during the warmer months, along with turtles that use the ledges as resting spots.

Water temperature ranges from around 20°C in winter (a 5mm wetsuit or semi-dry is worth it) up to 29°C in summer, when a 3mm shorty is plenty for most divers. Because much of the interesting diving here is current-influenced, a surface marker buoy and a guide who knows the local tide behavior are not optional extras — they're part of how the sites are safely run.

## When to Go and How to Actually Get There

**Season:** Kakeroma dives year-round, but the sweet spots differ by what you're after. June through October brings the warmest water and calmest seas, best for the outer drop-off sites and manta sightings, though this overlaps with typhoon season (roughly July–September), so build a buffer day or two into any trip during those months in case boats are cancelled. November through April is cooler and choppier on average but is the better window for the channel current dives and hammerhead activity, with clearer, less-humid conditions topside. Late spring (April–May) is a good compromise: settled weather, warming water, and fewer cancellations than peak typhoon months.

**Getting there:** The realistic gateway is Amami Oshima. Fly into Amami Airport (ASJ) — there are direct flights from Tokyo Haneda (around 2 hours 20 minutes), Osaka Itami/Kansai, and Fukuoka, plus connections via Kagoshima, which itself has frequent flights from Tokyo and Osaka and a short hop (about 30 minutes) onward to Amami. From Amami Airport, it's roughly a 50-minute drive south to the port town of Koniya at the southern tip of Amami Oshima. From Koniya, a passenger ferry crosses the Oshima Strait to Kakeroma's main port at Ikenma or Setouchi in about 15–25 minutes depending on the route; several crossings run daily. Many dive operators will coordinate a pickup at the ferry terminal if you've booked ahead, and some run their own boats directly from Koniya, skipping the ferry transfer entirely.

A rental car on Amami Oshima is worth having if you're staying overnight there before or after the Kakeroma leg, since public transit options thin out fast outside the airport corridor. On Kakeroma itself, most dive shops are small, family-run operations attached to a guesthouse, and they'll typically handle local transport for guests between accommodation, the shop, and the harbor.

## Booking, Gear, and Getting the Most Out of a Kakeroma Trip

**Booking:** Because operators run small boats with limited daily capacity, book at least two to three weeks ahead for peak season (July–August) and give a bit more lead time around Golden Week or Obon if your travel dates overlap. Most shops communicate by phone or LINE rather than online booking systems, so a short email in advance confirming dates, certification level, and logged dive count goes a long way — guides tailor site selection to group experience, and current-heavy channel sites are usually reserved for divers with drift dive experience or at least an Advanced Open Water certification.

**Gear:** Bring your own mask, computer, and reef-safe sunscreen if you have preferences — rental gear is available but limited in sizing since shops are small. A 5mm wetsuit covers winter diving comfortably; pack a 3mm or shorty for summer. If you're chasing the channel current sites, a reel-mounted SMB is standard kit here, and shops will expect you to know how to deploy one before joining a drift dive.

**Etiquette:** Kakeroma's reefs have stayed healthy partly because local operators enforce strict no-touch, no-glove policies and limit group sizes — follow your guide's buoyancy and positioning cues closely, especially over the shallow coral tables where a careless fin kick does real damage. Photographers should check with the shop about strobe use near cleaning stations, since repeated flash exposure can disrupt local marine life behavior.

**Stay and food:** Accommodation on Kakeroma is mostly small guesthouses (minshuku) rather than hotels, many run by families who also handle a boat or dive operation on the side. Meals are frequently home-cooked and included in the nightly rate, built around fresh Amami black pork, mozuku seaweed, and the day's catch — worth asking about in advance since some guesthouses only serve dinner if requested ahead of time. Given the ferry schedule and boat timing, a two-night minimum stay is the practical floor for fitting in two full diving days without rushing the crossing.
