## A Diver's Secret Hiding in the Kerama Blue

Most visitors to Okinawa never make it past the mainland's beach clubs, which is exactly why Zamami Sanninzuka Reef still feels like a discovery rather than a checklist stop. Tucked into the Kerama Islands National Park roughly 40 kilometers west of Naha, the site sits in water so transparent that local dive shops half-joke about "Kerama Blue" visibility stretching past 30 meters on a good day. What sets Sanninzuka apart from the dozens of other Kerama dive points is the combination on offer here: a sprawling, mostly intact hard-coral reef table that survived the bleaching events that hit other parts of Okinawa harder, layered on top of a manta ray cleaning station that draws repeat sightings through much of the year.

This is a site built for divers who want substance over spectacle marketing. It isn't a theme-park dive — there's no cage, no guaranteed photo-op guarantee plastered on a brochure — but the mix of healthy staghorn and table coral, resident reef sharks cruising the drop-offs, and the real chance of a manta gliding overhead makes it one of the more complete single-site experiences in the prefecture. Sanninzuka rewards divers who come with reasonable expectations and some patience: the manta sightings are seasonal and weather-dependent, but the reef itself delivers on almost every dive regardless of what swims by.

It also works for a wider range of skill levels than you might expect from a "manta point." The shallow reef plateau is calm enough for newly certified divers doing a supervised check-out dive, while the deeper ledges and current-exposed corners give advanced divers and underwater photographers something to work for. Snorkelers based on Zamami Island can even get a taste of the shallow coral tables on calm days, though the manta station itself is a scuba-only affair given its depth and current.

## Conditions: Dive Sites, Depth Profiles, and the Manta Point

"Sanninzuka" is really a cluster of connected dive sites rather than a single mooring, and local operators tend to split the area into three loosely defined zones that boats rotate between depending on current and group level.

- **The Reef Table (5–12m):** A wide, gently sloping plateau covered in branching coral, table coral colonies, and garden eel patches in the sandy breaks. This is where open-water divers and snorkel groups spend most of their time, and it's also the best spot on the site for wide-angle reef photography thanks to the shallow, bright light.
- **The Drop-Off (12–25m):** The plateau gives way to a wall and a series of boulder formations that attract white-tip reef sharks, large schools of fusilier and batfish, and the occasional eagle ray. Current here is noticeably stronger than on the plateau, so most shops require at least an advanced certification or equivalent logged dives before taking you to this section.
- **The Manta Cleaning Station (18–22m):** A cluster of low coral outcrops where cleaner wrasse gather, acting as a magnet for manta rays that circle in to be groomed. Sightings cluster in the cooler months and on days with manageable current, and no operator can promise a manta — anyone who does is overselling it. Guides typically have divers settle on the sand at a respectful distance and wait rather than chase, which both protects the animals and dramatically improves your odds of an encounter staying around.

Current is the main variable to plan around. Mild to moderate drift is normal on most tide cycles, and boats will often adjust the exact entry point or do a drift dive along the wall rather than fighting the current back to a fixed mooring. Water temperature swings from around 23°C in the depths of winter to nearly 29°C in high summer, so a 3mm wetsuit covers most of the year, with a 5mm or hooded vest worth considering for winter manta watches where you may be holding still on the sandy bottom for twenty minutes at a time.

## Season and Access: Getting from Naha to the Dive Boat

Diving at Sanninzuka is possible nearly year-round, but the experience changes a lot with the season. June through September brings the warmest water, the calmest seas, and the biggest crowds, including Okinawa's rainy-season swell in early summer that can cancel boats with little notice. Spring (March–May) and autumn (October–November) tend to offer the best overall balance: good visibility, fewer typhoon disruptions, and noticeably thinner crowds on the boats. Manta sightings are reported most consistently from November through April, when cooler, nutrient-richer water draws them into the cleaning station more often — ironically the opposite season from when most leisure travelers show up for beach weather.

Access starts at Naha Airport (OKA), the main international and domestic gateway to Okinawa and the hub almost every Kerama-bound diver passes through. From the airport, take a taxi or the Yui Rail monorail to Naha city (about 15–20 minutes), then a short taxi or bus ride to Tomari Port (Tomarin), the departure point for ferries to Zamami Island. Two ferry options run the route:

- **Queen Zamami (high-speed ferry):** around 50 minutes each way, running multiple times daily. This is the practical choice for a day trip or if you're staying only one night.
- **Ferry Zamami (standard ferry):** roughly 2 hours each way, cheaper and more comfortable in rough seas, but it eats into your dive day if you're not staying overnight on the island.

Once on Zamami, dive shops are clustered within a five-minute walk of the port, and most run their own boats directly to Sanninzuka and other Kerama sites — there's no need for a separate transfer once you've landed. If you're driving in Okinawa, you can park near Tomarin (paid lots nearby) rather than attempting to bring a rental car onto the ferry, since vehicle space is limited and rarely worth the cost for a dive trip. Typhoon season (roughly August–October) can disrupt both ferries and dive schedules with short notice, so build a buffer day into your itinerary if you're traveling from overseas specifically for this site.

## Practical Tips: Booking, Gear, and Reef Etiquette

Book your dive boat before you book your ferry ticket, not after. Zamami's handful of dive shops have limited boat capacity, and the days with the best manta-sighting conditions fill up well in advance during peak season (July–August) and during the winter manta window (December–February). Most shops offer two-tank morning trips that include Sanninzuka as one of the stops, plus gear rental for anyone not traveling with their own BCD, regulator, and wetsuit. If you're bringing your own gear, pack reef-safe mineral sunscreen rather than oxybenzone-based products — some Kerama operators will ask about this directly, since the islands fall within a marine protected area with active coral-monitoring programs.

A few etiquette points matter more here than at a typical reef dive: keep a few meters of distance from any manta at the cleaning station and resist the urge to swim toward it, since chasing is the single most common reason encounters cut short; avoid fin contact with the coral table on the shallow plateau, where even a light brush can snap branching coral that took years to grow; and skip flash photography close to any ray or shark, as strobes at close range can spook them off before the rest of the group gets a look.

For logistics beyond the dive itself, staying overnight on Zamami Island rather than day-tripping from Naha is worth the extra planning if your schedule allows it — it opens up an early first-boat slot (when current and light are often best) and spares you the ferry-timetable stress of a same-day return. The village near the port has a modest but solid lineup of minshuku guesthouses and small izakaya-style restaurants serving fresh Okinawan seafood, and almost nothing on the island requires a car, since the port, shops, and most lodging sit within easy walking distance of each other. Cash is still preferred at many smaller shops and eateries on Zamami, so don't rely entirely on cards once you're off the mainland.
