## Who Miyagase Lake Camp Is For

Miyagase Lake Camp suits travelers who want the feel of a remote lakeside retreat without sacrificing a short drive time from Tokyo or Yokohama. The reservoir, formed by the Miyagase Dam, sits in a forested valley at the edge of Tanzawa-Oyama Quasi-National Park, and the shoreline campgrounds here lean into that setting rather than fighting it. Families looking for a gentler alternative to Pacific coast surf camps will appreciate the still water, the absence of big swell or rip currents, and the grassy or decked tent sites that make pitching a tent (or settling into a glamping dome) far less technical than a beach pitch.

It's also a strong pick for first-time glampers. Unlike backcountry Tanzawa ridge camping, which demands real hiking fitness and weatherproof gear, Miyagase's lakeside sites are drive-up or short-walk-in, with on-site toilets, water points, and in many cases pre-pitched canvas tents or wooden cabins available for rent. Paddlers and canoe/kayak enthusiasts get flat water and a managed put-in, which is rare within a two-hour radius of central Tokyo. Anglers come for bass and other freshwater species, since Miyagase is one of the better-known reservoir fishing spots in the Kanto region. What sets this location apart from generic "lake camp" listings elsewhere in Japan is the combination of genuine mountain scenery, a managed water-activity program, and glamping-grade comfort options all within the same compact area — you don't have to choose between wilderness views and a decent night's sleep.

## Site Layout, Water Access, and Camp Conditions

The camp area is organized around the reservoir's northern and western inlets, where the water is calmer and shielded from wind compared to the open dam-side basin. Most operators here offer a tiered site system: standard tent plots on gravel or grass terraces close to the parking area, elevated wooden decks for tents (useful after rain, since lakeside soil can stay damp), and fixed-structure glamping units — canvas bell tents or small cabins — with beds, lighting, and sometimes heating or air conditioning already installed. Expect site fees and rental packages to vary widely depending on whether you bring your own gear or book a full glamping package with linens and a dinner barbecue set included.

Water activity access is the camp's defining feature. Canoe and SUP (stand-up paddleboard) rentals are typically available by the hour or for a half/full-day block, launching from a shallow, protected cove rather than directly into open reservoir traffic. Conditions are almost always flat-water — this is a dam-controlled reservoir, not a tidal or river system, so there's no current to fight and no swell to read. The main variable is water level: Miyagase's surface height fluctuates seasonally with dam management and rainfall, and in drier months (typically late autumn into winter) the shoreline can recede noticeably, exposing mud flats near some launch points. Always check with the camp operator the same week as your visit, since a low-water year can shift or temporarily close certain put-ins.

Fishing here runs catch-and-release in many zones, with specific rules posted at the boat ramps — bass fishing is popular but regulated, so confirm whether a day permit is required before casting. Swimming is generally discouraged or restricted to designated shallow areas, since the reservoir has sudden depth changes near the dam face; stick to signed swim zones if your site has one. For hikers, several trailheads into the Tanzawa foothills start within a short drive of the lake, making this a reasonable base for a half-day ridge walk before returning to camp for the evening.

## Best Season to Visit and How to Get There

Spring (April–May) and autumn (October–November) are the strongest windows for Miyagase Lake Camp. Spring brings mild daytime temperatures ideal for paddling and cherry blossoms along parts of the access road, while autumn delivers some of Kanagawa's best foliage, with maples coloring the surrounding slopes by mid-to-late November. Summer (July–August) is workable and popular with families, but expect higher humidity, afternoon thunderstorm risk typical of inland mountain basins, and busier weekend bookings — reserve well ahead. Winter camping is possible for cold-weather-ready visitors; the glamping units with heating make this season far more comfortable than standard tenting, and the clearer winter air often gives sharper views of the surrounding ridgelines, though water-level drawdown can limit paddling options.

Access is one of this spot's biggest selling points relative to Japan's more famous lake campgrounds. By car from central Tokyo, the drive is roughly 75–100 minutes via the Tokyo-Chuo Expressway or Ken-O Expressway toward Atsugi, then local roads into the Miyagase area — a realistic half-day trip if you leave by mid-morning. From Yokohama, driving time is comparable or slightly shorter. If you're relying on public transport, the nearest major rail hub is Hon-Atsugi Station on the Odakyu Line, reachable in about 45–60 minutes from Shinjuku; from there, local buses run toward the Miyagase Dam area, though schedules are infrequent and a taxi or pre-arranged shuttle from the campground is often more practical for the final leg with gear. There is no airport near the lake itself — the closest major air gateway is Haneda Airport, roughly two hours away by combined train and car/bus, making this far more of a car-trip or train-plus-local-transfer destination than a fly-in one. Travelers arriving from Narita should budget closer to 2.5–3 hours door to door.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette for a Smooth Stay

Book your site or glamping unit as early as possible for weekends from April through November, since Kanto-area families treat Miyagase as a go-to escape from the city and popular dates fill quickly. Many operators require online reservation and deposit through their own booking pages rather than walk-up registration, and some glamping packages bundle meals, so clarify in advance whether barbecue dinner or breakfast is included or needs to be purchased separately or brought yourself.

If you're bringing your own gear rather than renting, pack for temperature swings — lakeside basins in mountain valleys cool faster at night than coastal areas, so a sleeping bag rated several degrees below the forecast low is a safer bet, even in late spring or early autumn. A dry bag is worth carrying if you're paddling, since even calm reservoir water can splash gear during launches and landings. Water shoes help at the muddier put-in points, especially during low-water periods. Insect repellent is essential from late spring through summer, as the forested lake margins attract mosquitoes and other biting insects in the evening.

On etiquette: keep noise down after quiet hours, which many Kanagawa campgrounds enforce strictly given how close some sites sit to neighboring plots. Pack out all trash, including fishing line and bait packaging, since reservoir ecosystems are sensitive to litter and the camp's continued access to the water depends on maintaining good standing with local dam authorities. If you rent a canoe or SUP, follow the posted launch-zone boundaries rather than paddling toward the dam face or restricted inlets — these limits exist for both safety and water-management reasons. Finally, confirm fire policies before you arrive: many sites restrict open campfires to designated fire pits or raised braziers to protect the grass terraces, so a tabletop grill or rented fire stand is often the easiest way to still enjoy a proper camp dinner by the water.
