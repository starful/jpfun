## Why Niijima Stands Apart From Other Izu Dive Sites

Niijima is one of the smaller, quieter islands in the Izu chain, and that quietness is exactly what makes it worth the trip. Unlike Izu Oshima or the more crowded mainland Izu Peninsula sites, Niijima's shoreline is shaped almost entirely by pale volcanic pumice and dark obsidian-streaked rock, giving the underwater terrain a texture you won't find at typical mainland dive spots. The island sits directly in the path of the Kuroshio Current, which pushes warm, clear water up from the south and carries tropical fish species far north of where you'd normally expect to see them. Divers here regularly encounter parrotfish, butterflyfish, and schools of damselfish weaving through volcanic boulder fields that look more like a lava flow than a reef — because in a geological sense, that's exactly what they are.

This is a destination for divers who want variety without needing to fly to Okinawa. Niijima works well for intermediate divers who are comfortable with light current and boat entries, but there are also sheltered, shallow bays that are forgiving enough for newer divers or those doing check-out dives after certification. It's also popular with underwater photographers, since the contrast between jet-black volcanic rock, bright coral-adjacent marine life, and shafts of sunlight cutting through clean water makes for striking images that don't need heavy editing.

## Dive Conditions, Site Layout, and What You'll See Underwater

Niijima's dive sites are spread around the island's coastline, and which side you dive depends heavily on wind and swell direction that day — local dive shops make the call each morning. The most popular sites cluster near Habushiura Beach on the east coast and the calmer bays near Wakago and Mamashita on the west side, which tend to be the go-to choice when easterly swell picks up.

Typical visibility runs from 10 to 20 meters on a good day, occasionally better in late summer when the water is calmest. Water temperature peaks around 26–28°C (79–82°F) in August and September, dropping to a brisk 16–18°C (61–64°F) by February, so a 5mm wetsuit is standard for summer dives and a drysuit or thick semi-dry is worth considering outside peak season.

The underwater topography is the real draw. Expect volcanic boulder fields, swim-throughs formed by collapsed lava tubes, and rocky drop-offs that descend to sandy patches around 15–25 meters. Because the rock is porous and irregular, it creates countless small crevices that shelter moray eels, octopus, and nudibranchs, while the open water above the boulders often has schools of jack and occasional visits from larger pelagics when the current is running. Sea turtles are a realistic possibility, particularly around the calmer bays in summer. Currents can be moderate to strong at some outer sites, so most operators require an advanced certification or at minimum solid buoyancy control and drift-diving experience before booking those spots; the sheltered bays, by contrast, are close to shore, shallow, and suited to open-water divers.

## When to Go and How to Get There From Tokyo

**Best season:** July through October is the sweet spot, when water temperatures are warmest, visibility is at its best, and the tropical fish population is most visible. August and September also bring the calmest seas, though this overlaps with typhoon season, so it's worth building a flexible day or two into your itinerary in case a storm forces a schedule change. Late spring (May–June) is a solid shoulder season with thinner crowds and still-decent visibility, while diving from December through March is only for those comfortable in a drysuit and prepared for cooler water and rougher crossings.

**Getting there from Tokyo:**
- **By ferry:** The Tokai Kisen large ferry departs from Takeshiba Pier in central Tokyo, usually in the evening, and arrives at Niijima early the next morning — roughly a 9-hour overnight crossing. This is the budget-friendly option and doubles as a place to sleep, though it's weather-dependent and can be cancelled or rerouted during rough seas.
- **By high-speed jet ferry:** A faster jetfoil service also runs from Takeshiba, cutting the trip to around 3.5–4 hours, though it's pricier and still subject to cancellation in poor conditions.
- **By air:** New Central Air Service operates short flights from Chofu Airport (in western Tokyo) to Niijima Airport, taking around 35–40 minutes. Seats are limited on these small aircraft, so book well ahead, especially in summer weekends.
- **On the island:** Niijima is small enough that many dive shops offer pickup from the port or airport, and rental scooters or cars are available if you want to explore multiple dive site access points or the island's famous hot spring baths between dives.

## Practical Tips: Booking, Gear, and Island Etiquette

**Booking your dives:** Niijima has a handful of small, locally-run dive shops rather than large resort operators, so it's best to contact them directly a few days to a week ahead, particularly in peak summer months when boat space fills up fast. Most shops offer two-tank boat dives with English-speaking guides available if requested in advance, though confirming this ahead of time is wise since not every guide dives daily in English. Certification card and logbook are required for anything beyond the shallow bay sites.

**Gear notes:** Bring your own mask, regulator, and computer if you have preferences, but full rental gear including wetsuits, BCDs, and tanks is readily available on the island. Because much of the terrain is volcanic rock rather than soft coral, gloves are genuinely useful here (check first — some shops discourage touching marine life regardless) for steadying yourself near boulder fields, and a dive light is worth packing for peering into lava-tube crevices even during daytime dives.

**Etiquette and conservation:** As with most Japanese dive destinations, buoyancy control matters — the volcanic rock formations are fragile in places and easily damaged by fin contact, and local shops take reef and rock etiquette seriously. Avoid touching or chasing marine life, and follow your guide's lead on which crevices are safe to explore versus which house resident eels or protected species.

**Where to stay and eat:** Niijima has a small but genuine range of guesthouses and minshuku (family-run inns), many within walking distance of the port, and several include home-cooked meals featuring local seafood. After diving, the island's free open-air onsen baths — some perched dramatically on cliffs overlooking the ocean — are a popular way to warm up and unwind. Local izakayas serve fresh-caught fish, and it's worth trying the island's signature ashitaba (a local herb) dishes, found in everything from soba to tempura.

**Surface interval planning:** Because boat schedules and weather can shift quickly around a small island, build slack into your itinerary — a two- or three-night stay gives you a buffer for a missed or postponed dive day without derailing your return ferry or flight.
