## A Point Break for Surfers Who Are Ready to Leave the Beach Break Behind

Hebara North Point sits on the rugged stretch of coastline just north of Katsuura city, on the eastern shoulder of the Boso Peninsula in Chiba. If your surfing so far has been confined to the sandy beach breaks around Kamogawa or Onjuku, this spot is the natural next step. It's a true point break, where a rock-and-reef headland bends incoming Pacific swell into a cleaner, more predictable wave than almost anything on the open beaches nearby. The tradeoff is that it demands more from the surfer: reading a peeling wall off a point, managing a rockier entry and exit, and sharing a smaller, more opinionated lineup with surfers who know the break well.

This is not a beginner's wave. The takeoff zone sits close enough to exposed rock on bigger days that a shaky bottom turn can end badly, and the paddle-out requires picking a channel rather than just duck-diving straight out from the sand. But for a solid intermediate surfer who can already read a breaking wave and hold a line through a turn, Hebara North Point is one of the more rewarding stops on this part of the coast — longer rides, a more organized face, and a real sense of "proper" point-break surfing without having to drive all the way down to Shikoku or Miyazaki.

What makes it distinct from other Chiba spots is the combination of orientation and bottom contour. The point faces more directly into north and east swell than the beach breaks to its south, so on days when Kamogawa or the southern Boso beaches are flat or blown out, Hebara North can still be working. Locals treat it as a swell-window spot — not an everyday wave, but one worth checking specifically when the forecast shows a particular angle.

## Conditions: How the Swell, Tide, and Takeoff Actually Work

The wave at Hebara North Point breaks off a rocky point that curls swell around into a bay-facing wall, giving rides that are longer and more shoulder-driven than the punchy, short beach break peaks typical of this coast. On a good day, a clean swell wraps around the point and peels steadily toward the inside, offering a workable face for cutbacks, turns, and small sections rather than the closeout "catch and pop" rhythm of nearby beach breaks.

Swell direction matters enormously here. Hebara North Point wants north-to-northeast swell with a reasonable period — the kind of long-interval energy that typhoons spinning offshore in late summer and early autumn, or passing low-pressure systems in winter, push toward the Boso Peninsula. Southerly swell tends to miss the point's main pocket and leaves the wave smaller and less organized. Wind is the other variable to watch closely: the point handles light offshore or cross-shore wind from the west reasonably well, since the headland offers some shelter, but it goes choppy fast once onshore wind from the Pacific side picks up, which is common in the afternoons.

Tide is the detail that separates a good session here from a frustrating one. At low tide, more of the rock and reef structure is exposed, which sharpens the wave but also narrows the safe takeoff zone and lowers the margin for error on the bottom turn. As the tide fills in, the wave softens and becomes friendlier for intermediate surfers working on their turns, though it can also go a bit fat and lose shape on a big high. Most regulars aim for a rising mid-tide, which tends to balance wave quality against safety — enough water to cover the shallowest rock, but still enough bank in the swell to hold a face.

The lineup itself is compact. There's really one main peak off the point, with a secondary, mellower section further inside that's a reasonable place for someone newer to point breaks to sit and get a feel for the wave's rhythm before paddling up to the main takeoff. Because the peak is narrow, priority and etiquette matter more here than at a wide beach break — more on that below.

## Season and Access: When to Go and How to Get to Katsuura

The most consistent window for Hebara North Point runs from late August through November, when typhoon activity in the western Pacific sends long-period swell up toward the Boso Peninsula, and again through winter into early spring when passing low-pressure systems generate north and northeast swell along the Chiba coast. Summer (June through early August) is typically the flattest stretch, with small, inconsistent surf and more swimmers and beachgoers around the general area, though it's also the warmest water and most comfortable paddle-out if something does show up. Autumn typhoon swell is the headline season — this is when the point does what it's built to do — but it also means checking wind and swell charts carefully, since the same storms that produce the swell can also bring dangerous onshore conditions and rip currents.

Getting to Katsuura from Tokyo is straightforward and doesn't require a huge amount of planning. By train, the JR Wadai or Sazanami/Uchibo Line services connect Tokyo Station (or Soga, where many trains originate) to Katsuura Station in roughly two to two and a half hours, depending on connections. From Katsuura Station, the point itself is a short taxi ride or a 20–30 minute walk along the coast road, so a board bag on a local taxi is the easiest last leg if you're not driving.

By car, the trip is considerably faster and far more practical if you're bringing your own board. Via the Ken-O and Boso-Line expressways from central Tokyo, Katsuura is roughly 90 minutes to two hours depending on traffic, and the drive from Narita Airport is comparable or slightly shorter — around 70–90 minutes — making this a realistic add-on session for a surf trip that starts or ends with an international flight. Parking near the point is limited and informal rather than a dedicated surf car park, so arrive early on weekends and swell days, and be considerate about not blocking access for local fishing boats, which still use the rocks and small harbor nearby.

## Practical Tips: Gear, Etiquette, Booking, and Fueling Up

There's no formal booking system for the point itself — it's a free, open break — but if you want local knowledge before your first session, it's worth booking a half-day with one of the surf schools or guides based around Katsuura and Onjuku, several of whom know the point's tide and swell quirks firsthand and can save you a frustrating first visit. For board rental or repairs, Katsuura and the surf towns just south along the coast have small shops that can sort out fin boxes, wax, and leashes if you're traveling light.

Because the entry and exit involve rock rather than clean sand, reef booties or at least a sturdy pair of surf sandals for the walk down are worth packing, along with a board you're comfortable putting through a few scrapes — this isn't the place to bring a brand-new log you're precious about. A 3/2 or 4/3 wetsuit covers most of the autumn-to-spring season on this stretch of Chiba; water temperatures drop into the low-to-mid teens Celsius by midwinter, so anyone surfing December through March should plan for a thicker suit, boots, and possibly gloves.

Etiquette at the point is taken seriously by the regulars, precisely because the lineup is narrow and the wave is a shared, limited resource. Wait your turn, don't paddle around the pack to snake priority, and give way to anyone already established on the wave — this is doubly important given the proximity to rocks, where a collision carries more risk than at a sandy beach break. If you're newer to point-break etiquette, sit on the inside shoulder for a session or two and watch how locals rotate through priority before pushing for the main peak.

After a session, Katsuura town has a solid spread of casual seafood restaurants built around the local fishing port — the morning fish market area is worth a wander even outside surf hours — along with simple izakaya and noodle spots that suit a hungry, sandy surfer well. For an overnight trip, there are minshuku (small family-run guesthouses) and a handful of business hotels in Katsuura itself, making it easy to base here for two or three days and chase the swell across the tide cycle rather than rushing a single session and driving straight back to Tokyo.
