## Who Mikomoto Island Is For

Mikomoto Island is not a casual resort dive. It is a small, uninhabited rocky islet sitting in open water off the southern tip of the Izu Peninsula, exposed directly to the Kuroshio Current as it sweeps up from the Philippine Sea. That exposure is exactly why divers travel from Tokyo, Osaka, and increasingly from overseas to dive here: Mikomoto is one of the only places in Japan — and one of the few anywhere — where you can reliably encounter schooling hammerhead sharks in open water on a recreational dive.

This site is built for divers who already have strong buoyancy control, are comfortable with surface swells, can handle mid-water drift diving without a reference line, and are not rattled by blue water with no visible bottom. If your idea of a good dive is a calm reef wall with a guide line and 15 meters of flat visibility, Mikomoto will feel like a different sport entirely. If instead you want the adrenaline of descending into open water, watching the current and lookouts for shadows below, and surfacing not knowing exactly where the boat will be, this is one of the defining dive experiences in Japan.

What makes Mikomoto genuinely distinct — compared to other current-swept Japanese sites like Yonaguni or the Kerama Islands — is the combination of three things at once: a resident population of scalloped hammerheads that school in numbers from early summer through autumn, a true drift-diving format where the dive boat tracks your bubbles rather than you tracking a mooring line, and water depths and currents strong enough that local shops enforce genuine advanced certification requirements rather than just recommending them. Mikomoto is also a legally protected marine area, which has helped keep the hammerhead population and surrounding reef fish stocks healthier than at many comparable sites in the region.

## Conditions: Drift Diving, Depth, and the Hammerhead Window

Mikomoto sits roughly 1 to 2 kilometers off the Izu coast near Touji, Shimoda, and most dive shops run it as a boat trip of 20 to 40 minutes from the harbor depending on sea state. There is no shore access — every dive here starts and ends on a boat, and the boat captain's read of the current is as important to your dive as the guide's.

The core format is a negative or controlled descent drift dive. Divers typically enter together, descend as a group along a steep volcanic slope or wall that drops from the surface down past 20, 30, and in places beyond 40 meters, and then hold a mid-water position in open blue water at depths commonly between 20 and 30 meters, scanning the slope below and the water column around them. There is usually no bottom time spent kneeling on sand; you hang in blue water, finning gently to hold position against the current, waiting and watching. This is where the hammerheads are most often sighted — not hugging the wall, but cruising in loose schools through open water, sometimes a handful of sharks, sometimes considerably more during peak season.

Current strength varies by tide and by day, but divers should expect it to range from moderate to genuinely strong, and days are sometimes canceled outright when the Kuroshio pushes through too aggressively for safe diving. Visibility is usually excellent by Japanese standards — often 15 to 25 meters or more when the main current is running clean blue water through the site, though it can drop when counter-currents stir sediment near the island itself. Water temperature follows a warm-current pattern: it stays relatively mild even in winter compared to mainland Izu, but a 5mm wetsuit or drysuit is still standard for anything beyond late spring through early autumn, since dive times in deeper blue water cool divers faster than the surface temperature suggests.

Because this is a drift dive without a fixed line, dive shops run it with a strict buddy and group discipline: divers descend together, stay within sight of the guide, and surface together on a signal, deploying a safety sausage (SMB) so the boat can track the group's position before pickup. Solo exploration or wandering off to chase a shark sighting is exactly the kind of behavior that gets divers banned from future trips here.

## Season and Access: When to Go and How to Get There

The hammerhead season at Mikomoto generally runs from around June through November, with the peak concentration of schooling sharks typically reported from July through October, when water temperatures and current patterns align most favorably. Outside this window the island is still diveable and still interesting — the surrounding reef has healthy fish life, overhangs, and swim-throughs — but the signature hammerhead schools thin out or disappear, so divers specifically chasing sharks should target the summer-to-autumn window and book with a shop that tracks recent sighting logs.

Shimoda is the access hub, and it sits at the southern end of the Izu Peninsula in Shizuoka Prefecture. From central Tokyo, the most direct route is the JR Odoriko limited express train, which runs from Tokyo Station directly to Izu-Kyukou Shimoda Station in a little under 2.5 hours, with no transfers required. A cheaper but slower option is a combination of the JR Tokaido Line to Ito and then the Izu Kyuko Line onward to Shimoda, which takes somewhat longer but costs less without a limited express surcharge.

By car, Shimoda is reachable from Tokyo in roughly 2.5 to 3 hours via the Tomei Expressway to the Numazu or Nagaoka interchange, continuing south on Route 136 down the Izu Peninsula; traffic on summer weekends can add considerably to this, so a weekday departure is worth considering if your schedule allows it.

The nearest major airport is Tokyo Haneda, about 3 to 3.5 hours from Shimoda combining airport transit and the train or a rental car; Narita adds roughly another hour on top of that due to its distance from central Tokyo. There is no practical direct flight option into the Izu Peninsula itself — plan on routing through Tokyo regardless of your international departure point. Once in Shimoda, dive shops typically handle the final leg themselves, picking divers up from the station or a nearby hotel and driving them to the harbor for the boat departure, so renting a car for the last stretch is optional rather than necessary.

## Practical Tips for Diving Mikomoto Island

**Certification and logged dives matter here.** Most reputable shops running Mikomoto trips require Advanced Open Water certification as a minimum, and many ask for a recent log of dives in current or blue-water conditions, sometimes within the last six to twelve months. This is not bureaucratic box-ticking — divers who panic in strong current or lose buoyancy control in open water create real risk for the whole group, and shops here take that seriously.

**Book well ahead during peak hammerhead months.** July through September weekends fill up fast with both Japanese and international divers specifically chasing the shark schools. Shops based in Shimoda town can arrange two-tank trips, equipment rental, and sometimes package pickup from Ito or Shimoda Station — reach out directly in English or Japanese at least a few weeks ahead for summer dates, and check whether they maintain a sighting log so you can time your trip around recent hammerhead activity rather than guessing.

**Pack for genuine open-ocean conditions.** Bring a hood if you run cold, since holding still in blue water during a long surface interval or a extended mid-water hang loses heat faster than active swimming. A compass and a personal SMB are worth having even when the shop provides group equipment, since separation in open water with no visual reference is the main real hazard here, not depth or marine life. Seasickness medication is also worth carrying — the boat ride out can be choppy, and nothing ruins a hammerhead dive faster than starting it nauseated.

**Respect the marine protected area rules.** Mikomoto's waters are subject to conservation protections that have helped sustain its shark population, so follow your guide's rules on touching, feeding, or chasing wildlife exactly — this is a look-don't-touch site, and the hammerheads are wild, free-ranging animals passing through open water, not a captive attraction.

**Plan your post-dive stay around Shimoda's seafood and onsen culture.** Shimoda is a working fishing port as well as a dive hub, and the town's restaurants near the harbor serve excellent fresh seafood, including local specialties worth trying after a cold-water drift dive. Several small hotels and ryokan in town offer onsen hot spring baths, which pair well with warming up after a day of blue-water diving, and many are accustomed to hosting dive groups with gear-drying space and early breakfast times to match boat departure schedules.

**Have a backup plan for weather days.** Because the site depends on favorable current and sea conditions, trips can be postponed or canceled on short notice, especially around typhoon season in late summer. Building an extra day or two of flexibility into a Shimoda trip, rather than flying in and out on a tight single-day schedule, meaningfully increases the odds of actually getting in the water with the hammerheads rather than watching the weather from shore.
