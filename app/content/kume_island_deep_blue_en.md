## Why Kume Island Earns Its "Deep Blue" Reputation

Most people who fly into Okinawa head straight for the Kerama Islands or the reefs off Naha, and both are excellent. But divers chasing genuinely exceptional visibility keep quietly pointing further west, to Kume Island. On a calm day between late spring and early autumn, horizontal visibility here regularly pushes past 25 to 30 meters, and the water carries a saturated, almost unreal blue that gives the island its local nickname. It's the kind of clarity that makes a mediocre dive look spectacular and a good dive look like a magazine cover.

Kume Island is a good fit for divers who want the reef quality of a remote Okinawan island without the logistics of a multi-day boat charter. It suits intermediate divers looking to log genuinely clear-water dives, underwater photographers chasing contrast and color, and couples or small groups who want a quieter, less commercial version of the "tropical Japan" dive trip. It is less suited to complete beginners with zero open-water experience, since several of the better sites have mild current and depth that reward some comfort in the water, though local shops do run introductory and training dives for first-timers.

What makes Kume distinct from its more famous neighbors is the combination of scale and access. The island is small enough that a single day boat can reach multiple dive sites, a sandbar, and a swimmable reef flat all in one outing, yet the reef structure around it — especially near the Hatenohama sandbar system — is large, healthy, and varied enough that repeat visitors still find new topography. You get Kerama-level water clarity with a slower, more residential island feel on land, and noticeably fewer boats competing for the same mooring.

## Dive Sites, Depths, and the Day-Boat Rhythm

The dive scene on Kume runs almost entirely on a day-boat model rather than liveaboards, and that shapes how a typical day unfolds. Shops based near the harbor run two-tank mornings or two-dive full days, usually departing by 8:30 or 9:00 and returning by early-to-mid afternoon, which leaves time to shower, eat, and still catch sunset on the west coast.

The signature feature near Kume is the Hatenohama sandbar — a long, narrow string of pure white sand sitting in open turquoise water roughly 20 to 30 minutes offshore by boat, with no permanent land structures at all. Many day trips combine snorkeling or a shallow check dive on the fringing reef near Hatenohama with a deeper second dive on nearby drop-offs, where coral bommies, swim-throughs, and schools of reef fish are common. Depths here range from easy 5 to 12 meter reef flats suitable for Discover Scuba and refresher dives, down to 18 to 25 meter walls and ledges for certified divers.

Beyond Hatenohama, dive operators run sites around the island's southern and western points where current is more pronounced — these are the dives locals point to when visibility is at its best, since stronger flow tends to flush the water clean. Expect occasional mild-to-moderate current on these outer sites, drift-style descents, and the possibility of larger pelagic sightings (reef sharks, rays, and seasonal pelagics) in addition to the macro life and coral cover found closer to shore. Conditions are generally calmer on the lagoon side and livelier on the exposed side, so shops will shift the day's plan based on wind direction and swell that morning — ask your shop which side they're running before you book if you have a strong preference.

## Season and Getting to Kume From Naha

The practical diving season on Kume runs roughly from April through October, with the sweet spot for calm seas and warm water sitting between May and early September. Water temperatures sit in the comfortable high-70s to low-80s Fahrenheit (around 25–29°C) through peak summer, dropping into the low-to-mid 70s by late autumn, so a 3mm wetsuit covers most of the season and a shortie works fine in midsummer. June through October overlaps with Okinawa's typhoon season, and while most storms pass without direct impact, it's worth building a one-day buffer into any trip in case a system forces a boat cancellation.

Access starts in Naha, Okinawa's main hub. The fastest route is a short domestic flight: Ryukyu Air Commuter (RAC) operates several flights a day between Naha Airport and Kume Island Airport, with a flight time of only around 30 minutes. From Kume's small airport, most guesthouses and dive shops are a 10 to 20 minute taxi or shuttle ride away, and several shops offer pickup if you arrange it in advance. The alternative is the ferry from Naha's Tomari Port, which takes roughly 3 to 3.5 hours one way and runs less frequently — a scenic option if you have time to spare, but not ideal if you're tight on days. There is no train service to or from Kume; once you land or dock, a rental car or scooter is the easiest way to get around the island between dives, since public transport is sparse and most dive shops, beaches, and food spots are spread along the coast road.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette for a Smooth Trip

Kume's dive shops are small and personal compared to the bigger operations on the main island, which is part of the appeal but also means capacity is limited — book your dives at least a few days ahead, and further out if you're traveling during Japanese holiday periods (Golden Week in early May, and the Obon week in mid-August) when both flights and boat slots fill quickly. Most shops offer full gear rental, including BCD, regulator, wetsuit, and weights, so traveling light is realistic; just confirm sizing in advance by email or phone, since English-language support varies by shop and a quick message ahead of arrival smooths things considerably.

A few practical notes worth knowing before you go. Bring reef-safe sunscreen — Japan's marine parks are increasingly strict about oxybenzone and octinoxate runoff, and some shops will ask directly. Pack a dry bag for the boat ride out to Hatenohama, since spray is common even on calm days, and bring cash; many smaller shops and harborside food stalls on Kume don't take cards. For certification paperwork, carry a physical copy of your dive card rather than relying on an app, since connectivity on the boat and at some shops can be patchy.

On etiquette: day boats here are small and intimate, often six to ten divers, so punctuality at the harbor matters more than it might at a bigger operation — a late arrival can delay the whole boat. Follow your guide's buoyancy cues closely around the coral near Hatenohama's shallow flats, where fin contact is an easy and avoidable way to damage slow-growing coral. After diving, Kume Island soba and fresh Kume-grown seaweed are local specialties worth seeking out, and the island's small awamori distillery makes for a good non-diving afternoon activity if you're island-bound waiting out weather. Lodging is mostly small guesthouses and family-run inns rather than resort hotels, so book those alongside your dive days rather than assuming same-day availability, especially in July and August.
