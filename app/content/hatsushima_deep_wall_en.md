## Who Dives the Hatsushima Deep Wall (And Why It's Not for Everyone)

Hatsushima Deep Wall sits off the small volcanic island of Hatsushima, a twenty-five-minute boat crossing from Atami's harbor in Shizuoka Prefecture. This is not a beginner reef dive. The site is built around a near-vertical rock face that drops from around 18 meters down past 40 meters, with the most interesting structure and marine life concentrated below recreational no-decompression comfort zones for less experienced divers. If your logbook shows fewer than twenty dives, or your deepest dive tops out around 18 meters, this site should wait.

What makes it worth the wait is visibility. The Izu Peninsula sits at the edge of the Kuroshio Current, and Hatsushima's position away from the silty runoff of the mainland coast means the water here regularly clears to 15-20 meters, occasionally pushing past 25 meters on calm autumn days. Combined with the vertical wall topography, that clarity creates a genuine "big blue" sensation that's rare in Japan's more turbid coastal dive sites. You can hang at 30 meters and still see the surface shimmer above you and the wall disappearing into the gloom below.

The site rewards divers who come specifically for deep-diving practice and wall diving technique — buoyancy control along a sheer face, managing narcosis awareness at depth, and reading a dive computer under genuine multi-level profiles. It also draws underwater photographers chasing the deep-water fish life that doesn't show up on Izu's shallower house reefs: schools of larger amberjack, occasional passing rays, and a resident population of gorgonian fans and black coral colonies that only establish themselves below 25 meters where light penetration is low and current is steady. Casual snorkelers and open-water-only certified divers should look instead at Hatsushima's shallower bays, which several of the same dive operators also run trips to.

## Wall Layout, Depth Zones, and What You'll Actually See

The dive typically begins with a negative or controlled descent along a mooring line to the top of the wall at 16-20 meters, where the reef transitions from a sloping shelf into the vertical face. Local guides break the site into three working zones:

- **Upper wall (18-28m):** Soft coral cover, schools of damselfish and butterflyfish, and the best light for photography. This is where most guided groups spend the bulk of bottom time, since it offers the scenic payoff without eating deeply into no-decompression limits.
- **Mid wall (28-38m):** The namesake "deep wall" section — a sheer rock face undercut in places with small swim-throughs and ledges. Gorgonian sea fans and black coral appear here, along with occasional pelagics like yellowtail and, in warmer months, passing eagle rays. Currents can pick up noticeably at this depth, running along the wall rather than onto it, so drift-style profiles are common.
- **Lower wall (38m+):** Reserved for technical or highly experienced recreational divers running enriched air or deco-capable profiles. Few standard guided dives descend this far; it's mostly the domain of local tech-diving clubs and photographers after specific deepwater subjects.

Water temperature swings meaningfully with depth here — a thermocline around 20-25 meters can drop temperature by 3-5°C compared to the surface, so a 5mm wetsuit that feels adequate on the upper wall can leave you cold by the time you reach 30 meters. Guides plan ascent profiles with a mandatory safety stop at 5 meters and often build in a shallow second stop given the depth exposure. Because bottom time at depth is limited by nitrogen loading, most charters run this as a two-tank day: a deep dive on the wall first, followed by a shallower recovery dive elsewhere around Hatsushima or the Atami coast to extend surface interval benefits and let divers off-gas properly.

Current direction and strength vary with tide, so local dive shops check conditions the morning of and will sometimes swap the entry point or reverse the planned drift direction. This is a guided-only site for visitors — the mooring points, current patterns, and depth zones are known to local operators but not obvious to first-time visitors, and solo or unguided diving here is discouraged given the depth exposure.

## Best Season and How to Reach Atami and Hatsushima

**Season:** The Hatsushima Deep Wall dives year-round, but conditions differ sharply by season. Peak visibility and calmest seas run from late September through November, when typhoon activity has tapered off but water temperature is still a comfortable 22-25°C at the surface. Winter (December-February) brings the clearest water of the year — sometimes exceeding 20 meters visibility — but surface temperatures drop to 15-17°C, meaning a drysuit or thick 7mm wetsuit with a hood is standard. Spring (March-May) is transitional, with variable visibility as plankton blooms begin. Summer (July-August) offers the warmest water (up to 26-27°C) but also the highest boat traffic from recreational visitors to Atami and a greater chance of afternoon thunderstorms and swell from tropical systems, so morning charters are strongly preferred.

**Access:** Atami is one of the easiest coastal dive bases to reach from Tokyo. From Tokyo Station, the JR Tokaido Shinkansen reaches Atami Station in about 40 minutes; regular JR Tokaido Line trains take roughly 90 minutes to two hours depending on the service. From Atami Station, dive shops are typically a 10-15 minute taxi ride or a short walk plus local bus to the harbor area where boats depart for Hatsushima. If driving from Tokyo, the Tomei Expressway to the Odawara-Atsugi Road and then Route 135 gets you to Atami in about 90 minutes to two hours depending on traffic, with paid parking available near the harbor.

For international visitors, the nearest major airport is Tokyo Haneda, roughly 2 to 2.5 hours to Atami combining airport transit and the Shinkansen from Tokyo Station or Shinagawa. Narita is a longer trip, generally 3+ hours door to door. Most divers base themselves overnight in Atami rather than day-tripping from Tokyo, since morning boat departures (often 8:00-9:00 AM) are hard to make reliably via early Shinkansen connections, especially in winter when trains can be affected by weather delays.

## Booking, Gear, and Making the Most of an Atami Dive Trip

**Booking:** Reserve through one of Atami's established dive operators at least a few days ahead, especially for weekend trips in the September-November peak window, when boats fill with both local Japanese divers and regional visitors. Most shops require proof of certification (Advanced Open Water or equivalent, ideally with a deep-diving specialty or logged dives past 25 meters) before accepting bookings for the Deep Wall specifically, and many will ask for a check-out dive at a shallower Hatsushima site first if they haven't dived with you before. Nitrox availability varies by shop — call ahead if you want to dive enriched air, since it meaningfully extends safe bottom time at depth here.

**Gear:** A dive computer is mandatory, not optional, given the multi-level, depth-variable profile. Bring a backup mask and a whistle or surface marker buoy, since currents along the wall can carry a diver some distance from the boat's position on ascent. Wetsuit thickness should match season as noted above — renting locally is possible but sizing for larger frames can be limited, so travelers with less common builds should bring their own exposure protection. A dive light is worth carrying even in daytime, since it brings out the true color of the gorgonians and black coral in the mid-wall zone that otherwise reads as flat gray-brown.

**Etiquette:** Do not touch or grab the black coral or gorgonian fans to steady yourself in current — they are slow-growing and easily damaged, and local guides take this seriously. Stay above your guide's depth unless directed, since dive leaders here are managing group nitrogen loading carefully across the whole team. Log your dives and be honest about your comfort level with depth and current before descending; guides would rather adjust a plan than manage a diver in trouble at 30 meters.

**Stay and food:** Atami is a full-service onsen resort town, so post-dive recovery is easy — many ryokan and hotels have hot spring baths that are genuinely good for easing the mild fatigue that comes with deep diving. The harbor area and station district have abundant seafood restaurants serving fresh Sagami Bay catch, including the aji (horse mackerel) and kinmedai (golden eye snapper) the region is known for, both a fitting way to end a day spent looking at their wild relatives on the wall.
