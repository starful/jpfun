## A Quieter Alternative on the Shonan Coast

Ninomiya Beach sits on the western edge of Shonan, tucked between the better-known breaks of Oiso and Ninomiya's own low-key fishing harbor. Unlike Kugenuma or Shichirigahama, which fill up fast on weekends with a mix of longboarders, shortboarders, and beginner lesson groups, Ninomiya keeps a more relaxed, local pace. The wave itself is a straightforward beach break: sandy bottom, no rocks or reef to worry about, and peaks that reform reliably across a wide stretch of coastline.

What makes Ninomiya distinct within Shonan is the combination of consistency and forgiveness. The swell window here tends to catch groundswell wrapping from the south and windswell rolling in from typhoon activity offshore, which means there's usually something rideable even when nearby spots go flat. Because the sandbars are broad and the beach gently sloping, waves break gradually rather than pitching hard and fast — exactly the kind of environment where someone popping up for the first time, or working on turning a straight ride into an actual bottom turn, can get repetition without getting worked.

It's not a spot chasing barrels or drawing a competitive lineup. Ninomiya's identity is practical: a dependable, low-drama beach break that rewards people who just want to surf regularly rather than wait for a "good day." That makes it a favorite for Tokyo-based surfers building consistency, families introducing kids to the water, and traveling surfers who want a session that doesn't require gambling on conditions.

## Reading the Break: Swell, Wind, and Wave Shape

Ninomiya is a sand-bottom beach break, and like most spots of this type, the exact peak location shifts with the sandbars, so it pays to walk the beach and watch for a few sets before paddling out. Waves generally break both left and right off shifting peaks, giving beginners multiple options to angle their takeoff rather than fighting for a single fixed spot.

Swell direction matters more than raw size here. South and southeast swells wrap in cleanly and tend to produce the most consistent, evenly-spaced sets — this is the wind and swell combination locals watch for. During typhoon season (roughly August through October), Ninomiya can pick up significant size and power from storms tracking well offshore, so it's worth checking a real-time buoy or surf forecast rather than judging purely by the sky at home; a distant typhoon can send solid, well-organized swell even on an otherwise calm day.

Wind is the other variable to track. Mornings are usually the cleanest window, before the typical afternoon onshore sea breeze picks up and turns the face choppy. Offshore or light wind conditions groom the swell into clean, workable walls that are ideal for practicing turns; once the wind swings onshore, the same swell can turn into mushy, harder-to-read whitewater, which is actually fine — even good — for absolute beginners focused on straight-line pop-ups, but less fun for anyone trying to progress technically.

Tide plays a supporting role. Ninomiya tends to work across a fairly wide tide range because of its gentle beach gradient, but many regulars find a mid tide offers the best balance: enough water depth to avoid the wave closing out too fast on the sand, without the flatness that can set in at extreme high tide. Low tide can expose sandbars and create faster, punchier close-outs — manageable for intermediate surfers, less ideal for someone still finding their balance.

For beginners specifically, the appeal is the wave's patience: sections often reform two or three times as they roll toward shore, softening in power each time. That gives a new surfer multiple chances per wave to find their feet, and it means falling doesn't usually mean getting caught in a heavy, fast-moving section.

## When to Go and How to Get There

**Best season:** Ninomiya surfs year-round, but the most reliable stretch for consistent, rideable swell runs from late summer through autumn — roughly July to November — when typhoon systems generate steady groundswell and windswell across the Kanagawa coast. Early autumn (September–October) is often considered the sweet spot: water temperatures are still comfortable in a spring suit or shorty, typhoon swell activity is at its peak, and the summer beach crowds have thinned out considerably.

Winter (December–February) can still produce clean days, especially on offshore wind mornings, but expect a full wetsuit and colder water. Spring brings smaller, more inconsistent swell, making it a better window for absolute beginners who want gentler conditions to learn in without worrying about size.

**Getting there from Tokyo:** Ninomiya is roughly 75–90 minutes from central Tokyo, making it a realistic half-day or full-day trip.

- **By train:** Take the JR Tokaido Line from Tokyo Station directly to Ninomiya Station (roughly 70–80 minutes depending on the train). From Ninomiya Station, it's about a 15–20 minute walk south to the beach, or a short taxi ride if you're carrying a board bag and don't want to walk it.
- **By car:** From central Tokyo, take the Tomei Expressway or Route 1 toward Odawara and exit near Ninomiya or Oiso. Driving typically takes 60–90 minutes depending on traffic, and it's the more practical option if you're bringing your own board, as train travel with a full-size surfboard on the Tokaido Line can be awkward during busy hours.
- **From Shonan's core (Fujisawa/Kamakura):** Ninomiya is about 20–30 minutes west by car or a short hop on the Tokaido Line, making it an easy add-on if you're already surf-tripping around the Shonan coastline and want to check a quieter alternative to the main breaks.

There's no major international airport shortcut here — travelers flying into Narita or Haneda should plan to route through central Tokyo first, then connect via the Tokaido Line or a rental car south toward Kanagawa.

## Practical Notes: Gear, Etiquette, and Making a Day of It

**Boards and wetsuits:** A soft-top or funboard is the easiest way to get comfortable at Ninomiya, especially given the wave's gentle, reforming nature — there's no need to show up on a performance shortboard as a first-timer. Local surf shops in the wider Shonan/Oiso area rent boards and wetsuits by the half or full day, and several offer beginner lessons specifically because the conditions here suit instruction. Water temperatures swing from comfortably warm in summer (a rash guard or shorty is often enough) to genuinely cold in winter, when a 3/2mm or thicker full suit, boots, and a hood become worth considering.

**Booking and timing:** Ninomiya doesn't require advance reservations to surf, but if you want a lesson or rental gear, it's worth calling or booking ahead during peak season (August–October weekends), since local instructors and shops can fill up. Arriving early — ideally at or before sunrise-to-mid-morning — gets you the cleanest wind conditions and the least crowded lineup, particularly important if you're still learning and want space to fall without worrying about other surfers nearby.

**Etiquette:** Even though Ninomiya is less crowded than Shonan's headline breaks, standard surf etiquette still applies: don't drop in on someone already riding a wave, be aware of swimmers and other beginners near the shore break, and give lesson groups extra space since students often can't control their board direction as reliably. If you're new, sticking closer to the shoulder of the peak rather than the main takeoff zone is a considerate way to practice without getting in more experienced surfers' way.

**Food and staying nearby:** Ninomiya and neighboring Oiso have a handful of casual cafés and seafood restaurants a short walk or drive from the beach, many geared toward the local surf and beach community rather than tourists, so expect simple, good-value food rather than a resort-style dining scene. For an overnight stay, Oiso and Odawara offer a wider range of guesthouses and hotels than Ninomiya itself, and both are only a few minutes further down the Tokaido Line — a practical base if you're planning multiple sessions or want to explore other Shonan breaks like Oiso Nagisa or Chigasaki on the same trip.

**Facilities:** Public beach facilities are basic compared to the more developed Shonan hotspots — don't expect extensive board storage, showers, or rental infrastructure directly at Ninomiya Beach itself. It's a good idea to bring your own gear, a large towel, fresh water for rinsing off, and cash, since smaller local shops and rentals in the area may not always take cards.
