Most people picture Tokyo as concrete and neon, not a jumping-off point for wreck diving in warm, current-swept water. Yet the Izu Islands sit just offshore, and Aoshima — a small volcanic outcrop administratively part of Tokyo — has quietly become one of the more interesting wreck dives in the whole Izu chain. The site is a sunken cargo vessel resting in the channel between Aoshima and the open Pacific, now colonized by soft coral, sponges, and the kind of dense baitfish clouds that make wide-angle photographers grin behind their masks.

## Who This Dive Is For

This is not a first-timer's dive. The Aoshima wreck sits in a current-exposed channel where the Kuroshio Current runs close to shore, and the hull itself lies deep enough that Nitrox and a computer are effectively mandatory rather than optional extras. Realistically, you want an Advanced Open Water certification at minimum, ideally with wreck or drift specialty training, and at least a handful of logged dives in moving water. Local boat crews are used to briefing divers who already know how to read a current line and hold a safety stop in blue water — they are not set up to hand-hold nervous beginners through their first negative entry.

What makes this particular wreck distinct from the more famous wrecks around Izu Oshima or Hachijojima is the density of marine life stacked around a genuinely intact structure. The hull has enough recognizable form — bow, holds, encrusted deck fittings — to satisfy wreck purists, while the surrounding reef habitat brings in schools of yellowtail (kanpachi), striped fusiliers, and the occasional passing ray or hammerhead sighting during the warmer months. Underwater photographers who want both structure and biomass in one dive tend to rate this site higher than pure reef dives elsewhere on Izu.

## Site Conditions and Dive Layout

The wreck lies in a moderate-depth range typical of Izu Islands boat diving — expect the top of the structure to start around 18-20 meters, with the main deck and holds extending down toward 30 meters or a little beyond at the sand line. This isn't a technical-depth wreck requiring trimix, but it is deep enough that bottom time is genuinely limited on air, which is exactly why most operators here run Nitrox as standard.

Visibility is the site's best asset. Because the Kuroshio Current sweeps warm, clear offshore water directly past Aoshima, visibility commonly runs 15-25 meters on a good day, occasionally pushing further when the current is running clean rather than churned by swell. That same current is the site's main hazard: dive boats typically do a live drop directly onto the wreck, and divers descend fast along a shot line or the anchor line to avoid getting swept past the structure. Once on the wreck, the superstructure itself offers some current shelter, but exposed sections and any ascent through open water demand attention to drift.

Marine life congregates in predictable zones. The stern and prop area tend to hold resident groupers and moray eels tucked into the plating, while the open holds and the water column just above the deck are where the big schooling action happens — walls of fusiliers and jack species that part and reform around divers. Soft corals and hydroids have taken hold on the upper structure, adding color against the more corroded lower sections. Because currents can shift mid-dive, guides usually plan the route to work with the drift rather than against it, finishing near a natural ascent point where a safety stop can be held in the blue with a reel and float rather than fighting back to the anchor line.

## When to Go and How to Get There

Diving here is a warm-water, current-dependent game, and both factors point toward late spring through autumn as the practical window. June through October generally offers the warmest water — often 22-27°C at depth in peak summer — and the most stable visibility, since typhoon swell (the main disruptor, typically August through October) tends to arrive in discrete systems rather than as constant bad weather. Winter diving is possible on calm days but water temperatures drop into the high teens, and boat operators are far more selective about running trips given how exposed the channel is to swell.

Access starts in Tokyo itself, which is the real hub for reaching Aoshima despite the site sitting well offshore. From central Tokyo, most divers travel down the Izu Peninsula by car or the JR Odoriko limited express train (roughly 2-2.5 hours to towns like Ito or Shimoda) and then join a dive charter that runs boats out toward the Aoshima grounds, since the wreck itself is only reachable by boat rather than shore access. Some operators consolidate pickup from Shimoda or Ito harbors specifically for this run, given the extra crossing time versus a standard inshore Izu dive. If you're short on time, an early departure from Tokyo, a same-day return, and a two-tank charter is workable as a long day trip, though staying overnight in Shimoda gives you a buffer against weather cancellations and a shot at an early boat when conditions are calmest.

For those without a car, check that your chosen shop offers train-station pickup — many Izu dive operators run vans from Ito or Shimoda stations timed to the Odoriko arrivals, which removes the need to rent a vehicle just for this trip.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette Before You Go

Book through a dive shop rather than trying to arrange boat access independently — the Aoshima wreck run is a charter operation, not a walk-up shore dive, and reputable operators will ask for your certification level and logged dive count before confirming a spot, precisely because of the current exposure. Reserve at least a few days ahead in peak season (July-September), since weekend slots fill with both local Izu regulars and Tokyo weekend divers.

Bring your own dive computer and be honest about your Nitrox certification status — most shops here rent Nitrox tanks by default for wreck trips and will require the card to fill them. A 5mm wetsuit is standard for summer; step up to a 7mm or add a hooded vest for early or late season trips when surface temperatures drop faster than the water does. A reel and safety sausage/float are close to mandatory given the drift-diving reality of ascents here — ask your shop if they supply loaner gear or if you need to bring your own.

On etiquette: this is still a working wreck ecosystem, not a museum piece, so avoid touching or resting hands on encrusted surfaces, both for coral health and because corroded metal edges are sharper than they look. Follow your dive leader's line on the wreck exactly — penetration into any interior spaces, even shallow ones, should only happen with a guide who knows the specific layout and only if you're briefed and equipped for it. Log your no-decompression limits carefully; the depth and current combination here punishes divers who linger past their planned bottom time. Finally, budget time and yen for a proper surface interval and food back at the harbor — Shimoda and Ito both have solid seafood options near the dive shops, and a hot meal after a cold Izu current dive is less optional than it sounds.
