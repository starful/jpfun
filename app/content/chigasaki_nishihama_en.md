## Why Surfers Keep Coming Back to Nishihama

Chigasaki Nishihama is not the place to chase barrels or push your airs. It is the place where Shonan's surf culture actually lives: a wide, forgiving sand-bottom beach break just an hour from central Tokyo, packed on weekends with everyone from retirees on fish boards to teenagers learning their first pop-up. The wave here is soft, rolling, and wide-shouldered, which is exactly why longboarders have claimed Nishihama as their home turf. If you ride a log, a mini-mal, or anything with a single fin and some glide, this is one of the most satisfying beaches in the Kanto region to get repetitions in.

What sets Nishihama apart from other Shonan spots like Kugenuma or Zushi is its relaxed, almost small-town atmosphere. The beach sits at the mouth of the Sagami River, giving it a slightly different sandbar shape than neighboring breaks, and the view out toward Enoshima Island and, on clear winter days, Mount Fuji rising behind it, is genuinely one of the best surf-break backdrops in Japan. Locals treat this stretch of sand as a community hub rather than a tourist attraction: there are family-run surf shops that have operated since the 1970s, old-school izakaya a two-minute walk from the water, and a general lack of pretension that makes it welcoming for visiting surfers who just want to paddle out and have fun.

This is also a genuinely good learner's beach. The inside section stays gentle enough for beginners to catch whitewash and build confidence, while a bit further out there is enough push on a good swell to reward intermediate riders who want to work on noseriding, cross-stepping, or trimming. Shortboarders do surf here too, especially when a typhoon swell lines up the sandbars into punchier peaks, but the character of the wave rewards patience and flow over speed and power.

## Reading the Conditions: Swell, Wind, and Wave Shape

Nishihama is a classic beach break, meaning the wave quality shifts with the sandbars, so conditions can change noticeably within a single season or even after a big storm reshapes the bottom. The beach faces roughly south, which means it picks up swell generated by distant typhoons passing south of Japan as well as groundswell wrapping in from the Pacific. On a clean day with a moderate south or south-southeast swell, Nishihama produces soft, peeling shoulders that break both left and right off a peak, giving riders plenty of room to maneuver without the wave closing out too fast.

Wind is the biggest variable to watch. Mornings in Shonan are typically glassier, with the thermal onshore breeze picking up by late morning and strengthening through the afternoon, especially in summer. If you want the cleanest faces, dawn patrol or weekday early sessions are the move. Offshore or light wind days turn the beach into some of the best longboard conditions in the region: smooth, groomed walls that are ideal for noseriding and stylish, old-school surfing.

Wave size at Nishihama is generally modest compared to more exposed breaks like Shonan's Chigasaki Shinjuku Point or Osaki Point just down the coast, which actually works in its favor for the longboard and beginner crowd it serves. Waist-to-chest high is the bread-and-butter range most of the year. During typhoon season, roughly August through October, swells can push well overhead, and the beach break turns punchier and faster, attracting a shortboard crowd chasing hollow sections near the sandbanks closer to the river mouth. Always check the latest marine and typhoon advisories before paddling out during this window, since rip currents and sudden swell jumps are common.

Crowds are a real factor in the wave experience here. Nishihama is one of the most popular and accessible beaches in Shonan, so weekends and holidays from spring through autumn bring heavy lineups, particularly near the main beach entrances where the surf schools cluster. Paddling slightly north or south of the central access points, or simply surfing on a weekday, opens up noticeably more space and fewer waves lost to crowd traffic.

## Best Season and How to Get to Chigasaki Nishihama

Nishihama is a year-round surf spot, but each season brings a different flavor. Spring (March to May) offers mild water temperatures, building groundswell, and thinner crowds than peak summer. Summer (June to August) is busiest by far, both with surfers and with the general beachgoing public, since Nishihama doubles as a swimming beach, so expect designated surf zones and lifeguard restrictions during the official swimming season. Autumn (September to November) is arguably the prime surf window: typhoon swells deliver the most consistent and powerful waves of the year, water is still warm from summer, and the crowd thins out as the swimming season ends. Winter (December to February) sees smaller, colder, but cleaner conditions with excellent visibility and that classic Fuji-backdrop light, rewarding surfers willing to wear a thicker wetsuit.

Getting to Nishihama is refreshingly simple, which is part of why it stays busy. From Tokyo, the fastest route is the JR Tokaido Line from Tokyo Station direct to Chigasaki Station, taking roughly 60 to 70 minutes. From Shinjuku or Shibuya, the Shonan-Shinjuku Line runs straight through to Chigasaki in a similar time frame. Once at Chigasaki Station, the beach is about a 20-minute walk or a quick taxi ride (around 10 minutes); in peak season, seasonal shuttle buses or regular local buses toward "Nishihama" or "Southern Beach Chigasaki" also run from the station.

Driving from central Tokyo takes around 60 to 90 minutes depending on traffic via the Tomei or Shuto Expressway toward the Shonan coast, with Route 134 running directly along the shoreline. Parking is available in paid lots near the beach, but these fill up fast on summer weekends and typhoon-swell days, so arriving early is strongly recommended if you're driving. For visitors flying in, Haneda Airport is the better gateway than Narita, roughly 90 minutes to two hours total door-to-beach combining airport transit and the train connection through Tokyo or Yokohama.

## Practical Tips: Gear, Rentals, and Local Etiquette

Board rental is easy here. Several surf shops within a short walk of the beach rent longboards, soft-tops, and shortboards by the hour or day, and many also offer wetsuit rental, which is useful if you're traveling light or testing the wave before committing to buying gear. Lesson availability is strong too, with English-speaking instruction offered by a handful of schools that cater specifically to visiting surfers, making Nishihama one of the more accessible first-timer spots near Tokyo.

Wetsuit needs shift with the seasons: a spring suit or shorty is plenty from June through September when water temperatures sit comfortably in the low-to-mid 20s Celsius, while a 3/2mm full suit covers spring and autumn shoulder seasons, and a thicker 4/3mm or 5/3mm with boots and gloves is worth having for the coldest stretch from January through March, when water temperatures can drop into the mid-teens.

Etiquette at Nishihama follows standard Japanese surf culture, which leans more conservative and courteous than many Western lineups. Greet people paddling out, don't snake priority waves, and be especially mindful around surf school groups and beginners, since the gentle inside section is often full of first-timers who may not yet understand right-of-way. During the official swimming season (typically mid-July through late August), surfing is often restricted to designated zones marked by flags, so check local signage or ask at a nearby surf shop before paddling out, as rules can be enforced strictly by beach patrol.

For food and rest after a session, Chigasaki's beachfront area has a solid lineup of casual cafes and seafood restaurants, and the walk back toward Chigasaki Station passes plenty of options for a post-surf meal. Showers and rinse stations are available near the main beach entrances for a small fee, which is worth budgeting a few hundred yen for before heading back on the train. If you're staying overnight, Chigasaki and nearby Hiratsuka offer reasonably priced business hotels, making a two or three day Shonan surf trip an easy add-on to a Tokyo itinerary without needing to venture far from the city.
