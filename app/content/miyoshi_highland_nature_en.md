## A Plateau Escape for People Who Want Comfort Without Losing the Forest

Miyoshi Highland Nature Base sits on one of the elevated shelves above the Miyoshi basin, an area famous among Hiroshima locals for the "unkai" sea-of-clouds phenomenon that pools in the valley below on cool autumn mornings. Unlike the deep backcountry campgrounds scattered through Chugoku's mountains, this site is built specifically around the glamping model — canvas-and-timber cabins, elevated wooden decks, and pre-set fire pits — so the barrier to entry is low. You do not need your own tent, a car full of gear, or years of bushcraft experience to have a legitimate night in the woods here.

This makes it a strong fit for a specific set of travelers: couples on a weekend getaway from Hiroshima City or Fukuyama who want scenery without roughing it, families with young children who need a real mattress and a nearby toilet block, and solo travelers or photographers chasing the unkai cloud sea without committing to a full mountain trek. It is less suited to hardcore backpackers looking for solitude far from any facility, or to large groups wanting a huge shared car-camping field — the site's layout favors smaller, spaced-out units over a communal free-pitch lawn.

What actually sets this location apart from other Hiroshima-area campgrounds is the combination of elevation and forest cover. Most glamping sites in the region are either coastal (Setouchi-facing, hot and exposed) or valley-bottom (shaded but often humid and buggy in summer). Sitting on a plateau changes the microclimate noticeably: nights are several degrees cooler than downtown Miyoshi, breezes move more freely through the pine and oak canopy, and the elevation gives clearer sightlines for both sunrise cloud-sea views and stargazing after dark, since there is far less ambient light pollution than in the basin towns below.

## Camp Layout, Facilities, and What a Night Here Actually Looks Like

The grounds are organized in a loop pattern typical of Japanese glamping resorts: a central lodge/reception building housing the check-in desk, communal washrooms, and a small shop for firewood, snacks, and forgotten essentials, with individual glamping units spaced along gravel paths radiating into the tree line. Each unit typically includes a raised wooden deck, a canvas bell tent or A-frame cabin, real beds rather than futons on the floor, and a private or semi-private fire pit out front. Some of the larger family units add a second sleeping loft, useful if you're traveling with kids who want their own space.

Facilities lean toward comfort rather than survivalist minimalism. Expect flush toilets and coin or included hot showers in a shared bathhouse block, a communal kitchen or barbecue terrace for cooking dinner, and in many cases a small onsen-style bath or foot bath as part of the package — a detail worth confirming when you book, since it varies by plan. Firewood and charcoal are sold on-site so you don't need to haul your own, and rental gear (blankets, lanterns, cookware) is usually available for an extra fee if you're traveling light.

The forest itself is mixed conifer and broadleaf, which means the color palette shifts dramatically with the seasons — fresh green in early summer, deep shade in August, and a strong wash of red and gold from late October into November. Trails from the camp lead to a viewing terrace positioned to catch the sea-of-clouds effect when temperature inversions settle over the Miyoshi basin, which happens most reliably on clear, calm mornings after a cooler night. If you're here specifically for that view, plan to be up and at the terrace by first light, since the cloud layer tends to burn off within an hour or two of sunrise.

Evenings center on the fire pit. Because units are spaced with some buffer between them, you get a genuine sense of forest quiet rather than the shoulder-to-shoulder feeling of a packed municipal campground, while still being a short walk from real facilities if you need them.

## When to Go and How to Get There from Hiroshima City

**Season.** The plateau setting means this camp runs comfortably across a wider window than valley or coastal sites. Late spring (May–June) brings fresh foliage and mild days, though early June can bring rain from the tsuyu wet season — check forecasts closely if you book that window. Summer (July–August) is the most popular stretch for families, and the elevation keeps nights noticeably cooler than in Hiroshima City, which sits in a hot, humid basin of its own. Early autumn (late September through November) is arguably the best season overall: stable weather, the clearest chance at the sea-of-clouds phenomenon on cool mornings, and vivid autumn foliage moving through the forest canopy. Winter operation varies by year and specific unit — some glamping cabins stay open with heating for a snow-quiet experience, but always confirm winter availability directly before booking, as canvas-tent units in particular may close for the coldest months.

**Access.** The most practical route for most visitors starts in Hiroshima City. By car, take the Chugoku Expressway or Route 54 north toward Miyoshi; the drive from central Hiroshima runs roughly 1.5 to 2 hours depending on traffic and the exact plateau access road, which climbs from the Miyoshi basin floor. A car is genuinely the easiest way in, since the site is on higher ground away from train lines, and having your own vehicle also makes it easy to detour into Miyoshi town for supplies or to visit the Miyoshi Dinosaur Museum and Fudoin Kannon Falls area on the way.

Without a car, take the JR route from Hiroshima Station to Miyoshi Station (roughly 1 to 1.5 hours depending on whether you catch a direct or transfer service on the Geibi Line), then arrange a taxi or the camp's shuttle, if offered, for the final climb up to the plateau — confirm shuttle availability when you book, since it is not guaranteed on every plan. If you're coming from further afield, Hiroshima Airport is actually closer to Miyoshi than Hiroshima City is in driving terms; a rental car from the airport can reach the camp in well under 90 minutes, making the airport a genuinely useful entry point if you're flying in rather than arriving via Shinkansen into Hiroshima Station.

## Practical Tips: Booking, Packing, and Camp Etiquette

**Booking.** Glamping units at plateau sites like this tend to sell out fastest on weekends during peak foliage weeks (early-to-mid November) and during Japanese summer holidays (mid-July through August, plus Obon week). Book at least a few weeks ahead for those windows; midweek stays in shoulder season (late spring, late autumn) are far easier to secure last-minute and often cheaper. When reserving, check exactly what's included — meal plans, bath access, and firewood are sometimes bundled and sometimes billed separately, and this varies enough between operators that it's worth confirming directly through the booking page or by phone.

**What to pack.** Because units come with beds and a roof, you can pack lighter than a true camper would: a warm layer for after sundown (plateau nights run cooler than the city even in summer), a headlamp for the walk between your unit and the bathhouse after dark, insect repellent for the warmer months, and comfortable shoes for the short forest trails to the viewing terrace. If you're chasing the sea-of-clouds view, bring a light jacket for the pre-dawn walk — temperatures drop noticeably in the hour before sunrise.

**Food.** Many units include a barbecue set or fire-pit cooking setup, and the on-site shop typically stocks charcoal, drinks, and simple ingredients, but selection is limited compared to a supermarket. If you want a specific meal or want to save money, stock up in Miyoshi town or at a roadside station on the drive up — it's the last realistic point for a full grocery stop before the plateau road.

**Etiquette.** Because units sit close enough to hear neighboring fire pits and conversation, keep noise down after the general quiet hours posted at check-in — this is standard at Japanese glamping sites and taken seriously. Fully extinguish fires before sleeping or leaving your pit unattended, follow the site's rules on where charcoal ash can be dumped, and pack out any trash that isn't covered by the site's own collection point. If you're visiting during the cooler shoulder seasons for the cloud-sea view, be mindful that the viewing terrace can get crowded at sunrise on clear days — arriving a little early secures a better spot without crowding other guests.
