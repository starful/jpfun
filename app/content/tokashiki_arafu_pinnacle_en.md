## A Pinnacle Dive That Separates Casual Snorkelers From Real Divers

Tokashiki Arafu Pinnacle is not a beginner reef shuffle. It is a submerged seamount rising from deep water in the channel off Tokashiki Island, part of the Kerama Islands Marine Park, and it is built for divers who want the classic "blue water, big fish, dramatic topography" experience that draws people to Okinawa diving in the first place. What makes Arafu distinct from the dozens of other Kerama dive points is the combination of a true pinnacle structure — a rock tower that you can circle at multiple depths — with an adjacent drop-off wall that falls away into blue nothing. Few sites in this part of Okinawa combine both features so cleanly in a single dive.

The appeal is straightforward: descend along a mooring or shot line, hit the top of the pinnacle around 12 to 18 meters, and from there you have a choice of routes. Divers who want reef life and macro subjects stay shallow and work the coral-encrusted flanks. Divers chasing the drop-off experience swim to the pinnacle's edge, where the wall drops toward 30 meters-plus and pelagic activity — schooling barracuda, jacks, occasional grey reef sharks passing through the blue — is common on the right tide. This dual personality is why local dive shops rate Arafu as one of the "must-do" sites on Tokashiki alongside the more famous Aharen and Toguchi points, yet it stays less crowded because it requires a boat run out to open water rather than a beach entry.

This is a site for divers who already have some open-water experience and are comfortable with mid-water descents, some current, and dives where the bottom is not always visible below you. If your logbook is thin, go with a guide and treat this as a step up rather than your first Okinawa dive.

## Underwater Conditions: Depth, Current, and What You'll See at Arafu

Arafu Pinnacle sits in open channel water rather than a sheltered bay, and that exposure is exactly what produces its clarity and marine life. Typical visibility runs 15 to 30 meters on a good day, among the best you'll find in the Kerama Islands, thanks to the fast-moving, nutrient-poor water passing through the channel. That same water movement means current is a defining feature of the dive, not an occasional annoyance. Drift-style profiles are standard here: boats often do a live drop and pick divers up down-current rather than anchoring and expecting a return swim. Guides plan the dive around the tide table, and slack tide or a manageable running tide is what turns Arafu from "strenuous" into "exhilarating."

Depth profiles vary by operator and diver certification level, but a typical recreational dive works the pinnacle's plateau in the mid teens of meters, descends along one face toward the 25 to 30 meter range for the drop-off portion, then ascends back over the structure for a safety stop shallow on the reef top where soft coral and reef fish provide an easy, current-sheltered finish. Advanced or tech-curious divers with the right certification sometimes push deeper along the wall face, but this is guide-dependent and not the default plan.

Marine life is the reason people come back. Expect schools of barracuda and fusiliers stacked over the pinnacle top, jacks working the current edges, titan and other large triggerfish along the rock, and healthy table and staghorn coral gardens on the shallower flanks. Sea turtles are a realistic sighting on many dives around Tokashiki generally, and Arafu's blue-water drop-off is a spot where guides keep an eye out toward the open water for passing pelagics. Because of the current-swept, nutrient-rich water, gorgonian fans and soft coral color is noticeably richer here than at more sheltered Tokashiki sites — bring a wide-angle setup if you're shooting photo or video.

## Best Season and How to Actually Get to Tokashiki Island

Diving around Tokashiki is possible year-round, but the practical window most divers target runs from April through November, with peak water clarity and calm channel conditions typically in the late spring (May-June) and again in September-October, after the worst of typhoon season's disruption but before winter's stronger north winds pick up. Water temperature ranges roughly from 22°C in the cooler months to nearly 30°C in mid-summer, so a 3mm wetsuit covers most of the season and a 5mm or hooded vest is worth it if you're diving in the January-March off-season, when the water cools and fewer boats run pinnacle trips at all.

Getting to Tokashiki starts in Naha, Okinawa's main hub. From Naha Airport, take a taxi or the Yui Rail plus a short bus/taxi transfer to Tomari Port (Tomarin), about 20-30 minutes total door to port. From Tomarin, the Kerama Kisen ferry line runs to Tokashiki Port: the high-speed ferry Marine Liner Tokashiki takes about 35-40 minutes, while the slower car ferry Kerama takes roughly 90 minutes but costs less and carries vehicles. Ferries run several times daily in high season but are reduced in winter, so check the current Kerama Kisen timetable before booking a dive slot, and note that sailings can be cancelled in rough weather or approaching typhoons — build a buffer day into any multi-day Kerama dive trip.

Most divers do not need their own boat charter to reach Arafu itself: Tokashiki-based dive shops run daily two-tank boat trips that include the pinnacle as one of the day's sites, and they pick up directly from Tokashiki Port or Aharen Beach. If you're staying on Okinawa's main island and doing a day trip, factor in the early ferry departure from Tomarin (typically 9-10am) and the return sailing in the afternoon — a single-day round trip is workable but tight once you add a two-tank dive and gear rinse/dry time.

## Booking, Gear, and Etiquette for Diving Arafu Pinnacle

Book through a Tokashiki-based dive shop rather than trying to arrange an independent charter — nearly every shop on the island runs Arafu as a standard site on its rotation, and local guides know the tide windows that make the difference between a lazy drift and a fight against current. Because this is a current-influenced drop-off dive, most shops will ask for your certification level and recent dive log before confirming a slot; Open Water divers are usually fine with a guide, but shops may reserve the deeper wall sections for Advanced-certified divers. Reserve at least a few days ahead in the April-November season, and earlier still around Golden Week (late April-early May) and Obon (mid-August), when both ferries and dive boats fill up fast.

Bring your own mask and, if you have them, a properly fitted wetsuit — rental gear is available on Tokashiki but sizes are limited on a small island, and a mask that doesn't seal is a bad thing to discover mid-channel. A surface marker buoy (SMB) and whistle are strongly recommended given the drift profile; ask your guide beforehand whether the boat expects divers to carry their own SMB for the ascent, since pickup often happens away from a fixed mooring. A dive computer is essential rather than optional here, given the mixed depth profile between pinnacle top and wall face.

For etiquette: maintain neutral buoyancy near the coral-covered flanks of the pinnacle, since even light fin contact damages slow-growing coral that took decades to form. Do not chase or corner turtles or reef sharks if you're lucky enough to see them — hold position and let them pass. Follow your guide's signal on when to end the drift and surface; currents here can carry you further from the boat than expected if you linger. Topside, Tokashiki Island itself is small, with limited but good izakaya-style dining around the port and Aharen Beach area — fresh Okinawan seafood and awamori are the standard way to end a dive day, and most guesthouses can arrange dinner if you're staying overnight rather than doing the ferry day-trip. If you plan a multi-dive stay, book Tokashiki lodging early since rooms are limited and fill quickly in peak dive season.
